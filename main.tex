\title{A Consistent Vigour Network in Parkinson’s Disease: A Candidate Target for Brain Stimulation?}

\author[\firstAuthorLast ]{\Authors}
\address{}
\correspondance{}
\extraAuth{}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
\section{}
In motor tasks with repetitive trials, motor vigour varies substantially from trial to trial in people with Parkinson’s disease (PD). A key question is whether this behavioural variability reflects an unstable central vigour-related command, or whether a relatively stable neural command becomes more variable downstream as it is translated into movement. The existence of such a vigour-related network could provide a candidate target for brain stimulation rather than relying on less reliable, noisy behavioural trials. To examine this, we analysed individual trial fMRI data from 18 participants with PD who performed an overlearned, ballistic hand-squeeze task in OFF- and ON-medication sessions while receiving subthreshold galvanic vestibular stimulation (GVS) with different stimulus waveforms. Single-trial fMRI responses were estimated using the GLMsingle toolbox, and these results were then entered into a constrained optimisation framework designed to identify a network that was jointly active during the task, encoded behaviour, and demonstrated low variability in its activity over trials. This procedure identified a “vigour network” whose activity overall remained related to behavioural vigour but changed less noisily than behaviour itself. Anatomically, the vigour network included motor, striatal, cerebellar, thalamic, parietal, temporal-limbic, and orbitofrontal regions. Its limited overlap with the computed task-activation map suggested that vigour-related information was not simply a sub-network of task activation. After identifying this network, we tested whether it changed with GVS and medication. GVS waveforms associated with faster responses were mirrored by corresponding changes in vigour network activity, but not in the broader task-activation network. GVS also altered functional connectivity in both networks, but the pattern of connectivity changes in the vigour network was distinct from that seen in the task-activation map. We further found that dopaminergic medication strengthened local connectivity within the network. Together, these findings suggest that movement variability in PD may arise partly when a relatively stable vigour-related command is subsequently translated downstream into movement. The upstream network identified in this study may therefore be a viable target for neuromodulation therapies aimed at increasing motor vigour in people with PD.

\section{Introduction}
Motor vigour refers to how quickly and energetically an action is initiated and carried out. It is not a fixed property of the movement itself: the same person can perform the same action faster or slower across trials, and different people often express different levels of speed and force during the same task \citep{berret2018vigour}. fMRI studies have suggested that movement speed is related to a distributed set of cortical and subcortical regions, including motor and premotor cortices, supplementary motor area, basal ganglia, thalamus, parietal cortex, and cerebellum \citep{shirinbayan2019cortical}. Consistent with this, direct electrocorticographic recordings have demonstrated that cortical activity encodes both the direction and velocity of human arm movements \citep{anderson2012ecog}, while studies of the basal ganglia have shown that individual nuclei contribute to the regulation of movement force \citep{spraker2007basalganglia}. Collectively, these findings suggest that motor vigour is shaped by coordinated activity distributed across cortical and subcortical motor circuits.

Dopamine is central to motor vigour. Basal-ganglia output can change movement speed and amplitude without necessarily changing which action is selected \citep{turner2010basal}, and striatal pathway manipulations can speed up or slow down movement in a dopamine-dependent manner \citep{yttri2016opponent}. Reward and motivational value also influence motor vigour: actions associated with higher value are often initiated faster and executed with greater speed \citep{kawagoe1998expectation,reppert2015modulation,summerside2018vigor}. Parkinson's disease (PD), which is characterised by dopaminergic loss in basal-ganglia circuits, is therefore a useful model for asking how the dopaminergic state affects the expression of motor vigour.

Dopamine deficiency in PD is closely linked to bradykinesia, i.e., movements that are slower and typically smaller in amplitude. However, it is not simply that PD patients cannot move quickly. When speed is strongly encouraged, patients with PD can sometimes produce rapid movements \citep{mazzoni2007parkinson}. This points to a different problem: PD may make it harder to set and maintain the appropriate level of motor vigour from one trial to the next. Behavioural studies report larger short-term reaction-time fluctuations in PD \citep{macdonald2025capturing}, and earlier work found increased endpoint variability that could be partly reduced when participants slowed down or relied on visual guidance \citep{sheridan1990bradykinesia}. Motor output is also less stable: motor-unit discharge can become more variable in mild-to-moderate PD even when mean discharge rates are relatively preserved \citep{wilson2020motor}. Dopamine loss may therefore affect not only average movement features, but also how consistently those features are expressed \citep{lee2015dopamine}.

A key difficulty in interpreting motor vigour variability is that there can be several possible sources. Some movement variability is useful, helping the motor system explore actions, adapt after errors, and learn from reward; in particular, task-relevant movement variability can predict faster motor learning \citep{pekny2015reward,wu2014variability}. Some variability reflects the neural state before movement; preparatory activity in motor and premotor cortex can predict the speed of the upcoming reach \citep{churchland2006preparatory}. Another source is motor noise \citep{faisal2008noise}, especially signal-dependent noise, where larger motor commands generate greater variability and make fast or forceful movements harder to control accurately \citep{harris1998signal}. In PD, such downstream noise may be behaviourally costly: signal-dependent noise has been linked to effort costs in the more affected arm, and reducing this noise increased patients' willingness to use that arm \citep{salimpour2015altering}. More generally, noisy motor output can make the outcome of an action less predictable. When similar commands produce inconsistent outcomes, the motor system receives a less reliable teaching signal, which can impair learning \citep{therrien2016cerebellar}.


Since these sources occur together, behaviour alone cannot distinguish whether variable motor vigour reflects instability of a central neural command or noise added later in the motor pathway. This distinction matters for PD: if the central motor vigour command itself is unstable, then bradykinesia and inconsistency may reflect impaired neural coding. If a relatively stable central neural command still tracks trial-wise behaviour while behavioural output fluctuates, then part of the instability may arise downstream of that command or during its translation into movement. Distinguishing these possibilities requires measuring the neural activity. Trial-wise fMRI provides a way to estimate neural responses for individual trials \citep{mumford2012deconvolving,prince2022improving} and relating neural variability to behaviour \citep{garrett2013moment,haar2017individual}.

Defining a trial-wise fMRI network related to motor vigour would provide a new target for testing neuromodulatory techniques that may influence motor circuits in PD. Galvanic vestibular stimulation (GVS), whereby weak electrical currents are applied through electrodes placed over the mastoid processes, thereby perturbing vestibular afferent activity, is one such perturbation \citep{fitzpatrick2004probing,kwan2019neural}. Because vestibular signals contribute to posture, balance, and motor control, GVS has been used to test whether vestibular perturbation can influence motor behaviour in PD \citep{lee2015multifaceted,lee2021frequency}. GVS has also been shown to alter functional connectivity in PD, including connectivity involving the pedunculopontine nucleus and larger brain subnetworks \citep{cai2018galvanic,liu2021galvanic}. In PD, GVS effects appear to depend on the stimulation waveform and medication state. For example, multisine GVS shortened response time in an OFF-medication simple response-time task, but not in the ON-medication state \citep{lee2021frequency}. Separately, fMRI work showed that noisy and sinusoidal GVS increased the overall magnitude of deficient pedunculopontine nucleus connectivity in PD in a stimulus-dependent manner \citep{cai2018galvanic}. These findings suggest that vestibular stimulation can interact with motor-related circuits in PD, especially when the dopaminergic state is altered. However, they do not identify which brain system should be used to evaluate GVS effects on motor vigour. Most prior work has focused on predefined vestibular, gait, brainstem, or task-related targets, whereas motor vigour is likely expressed through a distributed network linking motor, basal-ganglia, cerebellar, and motivational systems. A data-driven vigour network could therefore provide a more appropriate target for determining the effects of different GVS waveforms to augment motor vigour.

Here we outline the detection of a data-driven vigour network in PD. The analyses were organised into two stages: first, we used trial-wise fMRI and behavioural data from a ballistic hand-squeeze task in PD participants, scanned OFF and ON dopaminergic medication while receiving different GVS stimuli, to identify and validate a vigour network. This stage used a constrained optimisation problem to ensure task engagement, behavioural linkage, spatial coherence, and low consecutive-trial variability, and then tested whether the resulting network was reproducible, less variable than RT, and anatomically distinct from standard task activation. Second, once the vigour network had been defined, we interrogated it to determine the effects of GVS and medication. GVS was used to test whether external vestibular stimulation altered the temporal profile of the vigour network, or functional connectivity within the network, relative to sham stimulation and to the task-activation map. We then determined whether the functional organisation of the network changed with dopaminergic state by assessing people OFF and ON dopaminergic medication.

\section{Materials and Methods}

\subsection{Participants and medication-state design}
Eighteen participants with Parkinson's disease were included in the final analysis: \(4\) female and \(14\) male participants, with a mean age of \(67.22 \pm 7.03\) years, range \(55\)-\(82\) years. Fourteen participants were right-handed, and four were left-handed. Participants performed the task with their dominant hand, except for one right-handed participant who used the left hand because of severe tremor in the dominant hand. Data from one participant's first session, the OFF-medication session, were excluded because one recording run was excessively noisy. Exclusion criteria included prior neurosurgical treatment for PD, other neurological or major medical conditions, dementia severe enough to preclude informed consent, severe levodopa-induced dyskinesia incompatible with MRI, and standard contraindications to MRI. Clinical severity was assessed using the MDS-UPDRS Part III \citep{goetz2008movement}; detailed medication-state and domain summaries are reported in Tables~S1 and S2 of the Supplementary Material. All participants provided written informed consent, and the study was approved by the Clinical Research Ethics Board at the University of British Columbia.

Participants completed two medication sessions on the same day: OFF-medication and ON-medication. They were instructed to stop dopaminergic medication at least \(12\)~h before recording. The session order was fixed across participants: the OFF-medication session was always completed first, after which participants took their dopaminergic medication and completed the ON-medication session approximately \(1\)~h later. Because OFF sessions always preceded ON sessions, medication effects could be partly confounded with practice, fatigue, time-on-task, or scanner drift.

\subsection{Behavioural task and GVS protocol}
During fMRI, participants performed a ballistic hand-squeeze task implemented in MATLAB with Psychtoolbox \citep{brainard1997psychophysics} while lying supine in the scanner and viewing the task through a mirror display (Figure~\ref{fig:task_analysis_overview}A). On each trial, a visual cue indicating reward value was presented for \(2\)~s, followed by a variable delay of \(2\)-\(3.5\)~s. A moving balloon then appeared as the Go signal. Participants were instructed to squeeze a mechanical bulb as quickly and forcefully as possible when the Go signal appeared, and successful responses were followed by a visual burst of the balloon. Reaction time (RT), defined as the interval between Go-signal onset and squeeze initiation, was used as the primary trial-wise index of motor vigour because it was the most robust behavioural measure in this dataset.

Each fMRI session consisted of two task runs, and each run included nine blocks of \(10\) trials. Most trials were rewarded, with low- and high-reward conditions counterbalanced across runs, and \(10\%\) were catch trials in which participants were required to withhold a response to avoid a penalty. Task difficulty was adjusted online by varying the response window based on recent hit rate, with the aim of maintaining accuracy at or above \(65\%\). A \(20\)-s rest period was provided after every three blocks. Behavioural quality-control and reward-related RT summaries are reported in Figures~S1-S3 of the Supplementary Material.

GVS (STMISOLA, BIOPAC Systems Inc.) was delivered continuously throughout each run using bilateral mastoid electrodes. Each run contained one sham block and eight active amplitude-modulated GVS blocks. The active waveforms crossed carrier frequency (\(75\) or \(125\)~Hz) with envelope frequency (\(0\), \(10\), \(16\), or \(25\)~Hz), producing GVS1-GVS8. The order of the nine stimulation conditions was randomised independently for each run. The GVS analyses were considered exploratory because stimulation was presented in short blocks, observations from the same participant were repeated, and no dedicated washout interval separated every adjacent block. Full stimulation parameters, electrode placement, thresholding, and block-order details are provided in the Supplementary Material.

\subsection{MRI acquisition, preprocessing, and trial-wise beta estimation}
Structural and functional MRI (Philips Healthcare, Best, The Netherlands) data were acquired on a Philips \(3\)T Ingenia Elition X whole-body MRI scanner upgraded to an MR7700 system (Philips Healthcare, Best, The Netherlands) using a \(32\)-channel head coil. For each participant, a T1-weighted structural image was acquired, followed by two task-based fMRI runs per medication session. Functional MRI data were acquired using an EPI sequence. The first three participants were scanned with a multi-echo EPI protocol, whereas the remaining participants were scanned with a single-echo EPI protocol. Full sequence parameters are provided in the Supplementary Material.

Image preprocessing was performed in FSL \citep{smith2004advances}. Structural images underwent bias-field correction and brain extraction. Functional images were corrected for head motion and susceptibility-induced distortion, high-pass filtered, brain extracted, denoised using ICA-AROMA \citep{pruim2015ica}, spatially smoothed with a \(5\)~mm full-width at half-maximum Gaussian kernel, registered to each participant's structural image, and normalised to MNI152 space. The scanner automatically discarded four dummy volumes before image data were saved, so no additional initial volumes were removed during preprocessing.

Task-related responses for individual trials were estimated using GLMsingle \citep{prince2022improving}, with HRF fitting, GLMdenoise \citep{kay2013glmdenoise}, and fractional ridge regression enabled. Analyses were performed separately for each subject-session, with the two task runs analysed jointly while preserving run identity in the design matrix. Go-signal onsets were modelled using \(2\)-s event regressors; catch trials were excluded because no squeeze response was required. The mean CSF time series was included as a nuisance regressor. After robust beta-value cleaning and group-level coverage filtering, the final group beta matrix contained \(91{,}769\) voxels. Detailed beta-cleaning thresholds, outlier rules, and coverage calculations are reported in the Supplementary Material.

\subsection{Stage 1: Vigour network identification and validation}

\subsubsection{Task-activation map}
To provide a conventional task-engagement reference, we ran a standard FSL FEAT GLM separately from the trial-wise GLMsingle model. All valid Go trials were modelled with one event regressor defined at the Go-signal onset, with duration \(2\)~s and amplitude \(1\). First-level maps tested the positive effect of task activity; runs were combined within subject-session using fixed effects, and subject-session maps were entered into a group mixed-effects model using FLAME stage~1 \citep{woolrich2004multilevel,beckmann2003general}. Group maps were thresholded using clusters formed at \(Z \geq 3.1\) with corrected cluster significance \(p \leq 0.05\) \citep{worsley200114}. This thresholded map was used only as a "task-activation map", not as the main model. The full standard GLM map and comparisons with GLMsingle activation maps are shown in Figures~S4 and S5 of the Supplementary Material.


\subsubsection{Constrained optimisation framework}
The central analysis was designed to recover a trial-wise neural projection related to motor vigour (Figure~\ref{fig:task_analysis_overview}B, C). The optimisation asked which multivoxel pattern produced a single neural signal that was task-engaged, related to trial-wise RT, spatially coherent, and locally stable across consecutive trials. This framing was important because the study question was not only where the task activated the brain, but whether a relatively stable central signal could be recovered despite behavioural variability.

The inputs were the cleaned single-trial GLMsingle beta estimates, denoted \(X_{\beta}\), and the corresponding trial-window BOLD, denoted \(X_{\mathrm{BOLD}}\). Columns represented trials or trial-window samples, and rows represented voxels. Because optimisation in the full \(91{,}769\)-voxel space was computationally impractical, both matrices were first reduced using expectation-maximisation principal component analysis (EMPCA) \citep{roweis1997algorithms}. The same EMPCA basis was used for the beta and BOLD matrices. An \(80\%\) cumulative explained-variance threshold retained \(14\) components, and optimisation was performed in this reduced space.

For a candidate weight vector \(w\), the beta and BOLD projection was
\begin{equation}
 y_{\beta}=w^{\top}X_{\beta}, 
 \qquad
 y_{BOLD}=w^{\top}X_{BOLD},
\end{equation}
where \(y_{\beta}\) is one scalar neural value per trial. The same weight vector was applied to \(X_{\mathrm{BOLD}}\) to quantify over-trial instability in the BOLD signal \(y_{BOLD}\). We refer to this \(y_{\beta}\) and \(y_{BOLD}\) throughout the paper as the "projection". The EMPCA weights were then projected back to voxel space, resulting in a voxel-weight map. Voxels in the upper tail of the map were used to define the vigour network, as described below.

The objective combined four penalty terms with one RT-alignment term. The four penalty terms were defined as follows. \(L_{\mathrm{task}}\) is the task-engagement penalty: it assigns larger penalties to weakly task-engaged components and smaller penalties to components with stronger task-related beta responses. \(L_{\mathrm{BOLD}}\) is the consecutive-trial BOLD instability penalty, formed from squared differences between adjacent projected BOLD feature trials. \(L_{\beta}\) is the corresponding consecutive-trial beta instability penalty, formed from squared differences between adjacent single-trial beta feature vectors. \(L_{\mathrm{smooth}}\) is the spatial smoothness penalty, obtained from the voxel-neighbour graph Laplacian. Consecutive-trial variability was used instead of total variance because it preserves trial order: it penalises abrupt local changes while allowing slower changes across task context, block, or run. Run and block boundaries were excluded from the BOLD and beta instability penalties because adjacent samples across those boundaries do not represent continuous local dynamics.

These four penalties were combined into a single total loss,
\begin{equation}
L_{\mathrm{total}}(w)=
\alpha_{\mathrm{task}}L_{\mathrm{task}}(w)+
\alpha_{\mathrm{BOLD}}L_{\mathrm{BOLD}}(w)+
\alpha_{\beta}L_{\beta}(w)+
\alpha_{\mathrm{smooth}}L_{\mathrm{smooth}}(w) .
\end{equation}
Here, \(\alpha_{\mathrm{task}}\), \(\alpha_{\mathrm{BOLD}}\), \(\alpha_{\beta}\), and \(\alpha_{\mathrm{smooth}}\) control the relative contributions of task engagement, BOLD local stability, beta local stability, and spatial smoothness, respectively. The behavioural relevance penalty was represented by \(L_{\mathrm{RT}}\), which captures alignment between the projected beta signal and RT. This behavioural penalty term was normalised by \(L_{\mathrm{var}}\), the beta-signal variance matrix, so that the objective favoured RT alignment rather than simply selecting high-variance components. The final optimisation problem was
\begin{equation}
\min_{\|w\|_2=1}
\frac{\gamma L_{\mathrm{total}}(w) - L_{\mathrm{RT}} (w)}
{L_{\mathrm{var}}(w)} .
\end{equation}
The negative sign before \(L_{\mathrm{RT}}\) reflects that RT alignment was maximised, whereas the other terms were penalised. The scalar \(\gamma\) controlled the overall balance between regularisation and behavioural relevance. These were graded criteria, not hard exclusion rules: a feature was not discarded because it was weak on one term; the final solution was the projection that best balanced all criteria. All defined penalty terms are in quadratic form. Their full definitions and derivation are provided in Appendix~A.

The constrained optimisation was solved using Sequential Least Squares Programming (SLSQP) \citep{nocedal2006numerical,kraft1988software}. Hyperparameters were selected with a coarse-to-fine cross-validation procedure using contiguous folds. We first tested a wide range of candidate settings and then tested smaller proportional changes around the best setting from this first search. Model selection used the held-out absolute correlation between \(y_{\beta}\) and RT. The final setting was chosen using held-out RT correlation, stability across folds, and small train-test differences. For each fold, the model was fit on the training data and projected back to voxel space. The fold-specific voxel-weight maps were then averaged to obtain the final voxel-weight map and trial-wise projection. Medication state and GVS condition were not used to define the vigour network; they were analysed only after the vigour network had been estimated.


\subsubsection{Network definition and validation}
The vigour network was defined as voxels with weights above the \(90\)th percentile. This threshold was chosen because it provided a stable compromise between spatial specificity and anatomical coverage: lower thresholds retained substantially more voxels, whereas stricter thresholds began to remove several anatomical groups. Threshold robustness is shown in Figure~S6 of the Supplementary Material.

We validated the model in three ways. (i)We assessed the reproducibility of the optimised voxel-weight maps across cross-validation trial folds using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC). (ii) We compared the consecutive-trial variability of the motor vigour projection with the consecutive-trial variability of RT. The normalised consecutive-trial variability metric was
\begin{equation}
\sum_i \frac{(x_i-x_{i+1})^2}{x_i^2+x_{i+1}^2},
\end{equation}
where \(x_i\) denotes the value of the signal on trial \(i\), using either the projection or RT depending on the comparison. The summation index \(i\) therefore runs over adjacent valid trial pairs after excluding run and block boundaries. Inferential statistics used subject-level summaries. (iii) We tested whether vigour network voxels were less variable than a motor-area control pool. The control pool was defined from bilateral motor-area atlas labels after removing selected voxels, and \(1{,}000\) size-matched samples were drawn without replacement. The control distribution was matched to the vigour network by sample size only; samples were not additionally matched by hemisphere, ROI size, spatial location, or local smoothness.

We next assessed whether the defined vigour network was anatomically distinct from the standard task-activation map. Voxel-wise overlap between the vigour network and the task-activation map was quantified using the number of shared voxels, the Dice coefficient \citep{elin2022new}, and the Jaccard index \citep{maitra2010re}. ROI-level anatomical coverage was summarised as the percentage of each atlas-defined ROI occupied by the vigour network, the task-activation map, and their same-voxel overlap.

\subsection{Stage 2: Network characterisation and modulation}
These analyses were performed after the vigour network had been defined. We specifically investigated the effects of different GVS stimuli and dopaminergic medication on the defined network.


\subsubsection{Exploratory GVS-related network analysis}
We first tested whether GVS modulated behavioural vigour. Trial-wise reaction-time data were extracted for each subject, session, run, and stimulation block. The sham condition was used as the reference condition, and the eight GVS waveforms were labelled GVS1-GVS8. Reaction time was analysed as RT, so that lower values reflected faster responding and therefore greater behavioural vigour. To reduce between-subject scaling differences, RT values were z-scored within each subject and session.

Separate mixed-effects models were fitted for the OFF-medication and ON-medication sessions. For each medication session, two nested models were compared. The reduced model included run as a fixed effect, accounting for general differences between runs. The full model included both run and GVS stimuli as fixed effects, with sham as the reference condition. A likelihood-ratio test was used to determine whether adding a GVS stimulus explained additional variance in inverse RT beyond run effects. We then examined the individual GVS coefficients from the full model. Because sham was the reference condition, each coefficient represented the difference between one active GVS waveform and sham. The eight active-GVS-versus-sham p-values were corrected separately within each medication session using Benjamini-Hochberg false-discovery-rate correction \citep{benjamini1995controlling}.

We next examined whether GVS altered the temporal profile of the vigour network signal. As described in Section~2.4.2, trial-wise BOLD time courses were projected onto the vigour network for each trial. For each subject, medication session, run, and GVS stimulus, projected signals were averaged within a stimulation block. The sham block from the same run served as the within-run reference for each active GVS stimulus. This within-run comparison was used to reduce the influence of run-level differences unrelated to stimulation. For visual inspection, subject-level condition time courses were plotted separately for the OFF-medication and ON-medication sessions. These plots included the sham condition and all eight active GVS stimuli. The purpose of these time-course plots was descriptive: they were used to inspect whether different GVS waveforms appeared to change the shape, amplitude, or temporal evolution of the projected motor-vigour signal.
% As described in Section~2.4.2, trial-wise BOLD time courses were projected onto the vigour network, yielding one projected signal time course, \(y\), for each trial.

To test whether any observed GVS-related changes were specific to the vigour network, the same procedure was repeated for the task-activation map signal. In this control analysis, the projected motor-vigour signal was replaced by the task-activation map BOLD signal, while the same GVS labels, medication-session split, within-run sham reference, and plotting approach were retained. This allowed us to compare whether stimulation effects were expressed preferentially in the vigour network or whether similar changes were also present in a task-activation map signal.

To quantify GVS-related changes in the shape of the projected time courses, four predefined features were extracted from each trial-level signal: late-early response difference, linear slope, maximum absolute baseline response, and peak-to-peak amplitude. The late-early response difference was calculated as the mean of the last three TRs minus the mean of the initial three TRs. This feature indicated whether the signal tended to increase or decrease across the trial. The linear slope was obtained by fitting a least-squares regression line to the entire trial time course. It provided a second, complementary measure of gradual change over time. The maximum absolute baseline response was defined as the largest absolute deviation from the value at the first time point, reflecting the strongest departure from the starting level. Finally, the peak-to-peak amplitude was the difference between the maximum and minimum signal values across the trial, representing the overall dynamic range of the response. For each feature, values were first averaged within subject, medication session, run, and GVS stimulus. Each active GVS stimulus condition was then paired with the sham condition from the same subject, medication session, and run. Active-minus-sham feature differences were computed at the run level and then averaged within subject. For each active GVS stimulus and each feature, subject-level active-minus-sham differences were tested against zero using a two-sided sign-flip permutation test. Confidence intervals were estimated using bootstrap resampling. P-values were corrected across the GVS-by-feature family using Benjamini-Hochberg FDR correction \citep{benjamini1995controlling}. The same feature-quantification procedure was applied to both the vigour network and the task-activation map.

Finally, we examined whether GVS modulated functional connectivity within the same ROI sets. For each subject, medication session, run, and GVS stimulus, trial-wise ROI beta estimates were grouped by stimulation block. The sham block served as the within-run baseline, and each GVS waveform was compared with the matched sham block from the same run. Connectivity was computed using the two edge metrics: Pearson correlation and mutual information. For each GVS block, active-sham edge changes were computed separately for each metric. Thus, the GVS analysis asked whether stimulation changed between-ROI coupling relative to the matched sham condition. Block-level edge-change values were pooled across subjects, medication sessions, runs, and active GVS stimuli. Each edge was tested against zero using a two-sided sign-flip permutation test with \(5{,}000\) permutations, followed by false-discovery-rate (FDR) correction within metric and analysis scope \citep{benjamini1995controlling}. Because this analysis included repeated block-level observations from participants and each block contained few trials, GVS findings were treated as exploratory.

\subsubsection{Medication-state network analysis}
We also characterised the vigour network by examining its response to dopaminergic medication. Medication state was not explicitly used during network optimisation, so this analysis provided an independent characterisation of the vigour network. Only participants with data in both medication sessions were included in paired analyses (\(n=17\)). Selected voxels were assigned to atlas-defined ROIs, and the optimised voxel weights were used to compute a weighted mean beta series for each ROI. The same connectivity analysis was repeated using the standard task-activation map as a reference network. Unlike the vigour network, the task-activation map did not contain voxel weights; therefore, ROI beta series for this reference network were computed as unweighted averages across task-active voxels within each ROI.

We used two complementary undirected functional connectivity metrics to assess medication effects: (i) mutual information, which tests whether the activity level of one ROI provides information about the activity level of another ROI beyond chance, including dependencies that may not be purely linear, and (ii) Pearson correlation, which captures linear co-fluctuation \citep{cover2006elements}. Mutual information was estimated after discretising each beta series into three rank-based quantile bins. Because both metrics are symmetric, related effects were interpreted as changes in statistical coupling rather than causal influence.

We tested whether medication changed the overall ROI-to-ROI connectivity beyond within-session run-to-run variability. For each participant, medication session, run, and connectivity metric, the upper-triangular ROI-pair values from the connectivity matrix were vectorized into an edge vector. Network-pattern dissimilarity between two runs was quantified using correlation distance, defined as \(1-|r|\), where \(r\) was the Pearson correlation between the two edge vectors. Smaller values, therefore, indicated more similar edge patterns, whereas larger values indicated greater dissimilarity. For each participant and connectivity metric, within-state run-to-run distance was calculated as the mean of the OFF run-1 versus OFF run-2 distance and the ON run-1 versus ON run-2 distance. Cross-state distance was calculated as the mean of the four OFF-run to ON-run distances. Medication-related excess distance was then defined as cross-state distance minus within-state run-to-run distance. Positive values indicated that the edge pattern differed more between medication sessions than expected from run-to-run variability alone. Group inference tested whether subject-level excess distance was greater than zero using a sign-flip permutation test. Cohen's \(d\) was computed as the mean excess distance divided by its subject-level standard deviation (Figure~S9 of the Supplementary Material).

We also tested whether medication preferentially affected local within-ROI coupling or distributed between-ROI coupling. Between-ROI connectivity was the average Fisher-\(z\)-transformed Pearson correlation between all pairs of ROI beta series. Within-ROI connectivity was the average Fisher-\(z\)-transformed Pearson correlation between voxel beta series inside each ROI; within-ROI values were averaged equally across ROIs so that larger ROIs did not dominate. The primary contrast tested whether the ON–OFF change in within-ROI connectivity was larger than the ON–OFF change in between-ROI connectivity.



\section{Results}
We present the Results in two stages. Stage 1 describes the  identification and validation of the vigour network, including  its stability, behavioural relevance, and distinction from standard task activation. Stage 2 examines whether this network is modulated by dopaminergic medication and by different GVS stimuli.

\subsection{Behavioural dataset and reaction-time profile}
After trial- and run-level quality control, the final dataset included \(18\) participants, \(35\) sessions, \(70\) runs, and \(5{,}463\) Go trials. Mean RT was \(387.2 \pm 65.8\)~ms in the OFF-medication session and \(375.5 \pm 64.7\)~ms in the ON-medication session, with only a small paired medication effect (Figure~S2 of the Supplementary Material). Notably, although mean RT was lower ON medication, consecutive-trial RT variability was higher ON than OFF ($4.04$ versus $3.50$). Reward effects varied across participants, with some showing faster responses in high-reward trials and others showing minimal or opposite reward-related changes (Figure~S3 of the Supplementary Material).

\subsection{Stage 1: Identification and validation of the vigour network}
This stage addressed the central question of whether a reproducible, behaviour-linked neural network could be recovered that was more stable than RT and distinct from standard task activation.

\subsubsection{The optimised projection was reproducible and less variable than RT}
The optimised voxel-weight map was highly reproducible across cross-validation folds (ICC \(=0.978\)). At the subject level, the vigour-network projection showed lower consecutive-trial variability than RT, with a positive behaviour-minus-projection difference (mean difference \(= 7.8\), \(95\%\) CI \([2.9, 12.7]\), paired \(p = 0.004\); Figure~\ref{fig:optimization_validation}A). Thus, the optimised projection preserved behavioural relevance while varying more smoothly than RT.

Selected voxels also showed lower consecutive-trial beta variability than size-matched samples from the non-selected motor-area control pool (vigour network mean \(=2.50\); control mean \(=3.46\); \(95\%\)CI \([2.76,4.38]\);\(p = 0.001\) from \(1{,}000\) resamples; Figure~\ref{fig:optimization_validation}B).

Together, these analyses indicated that the model recovered a behaviour-linked neural projection with lower consecutive-trial variability than RT. Because reduced consecutive‑trial variability was part of the optimisation objective, this result serves as a model verification rather than independent evidence of neural stability.
%supporting the presence of a stable central vigour-related component in PD.

\subsubsection{The vigour network was anatomically distributed}
The vigour network comprised \(8{,}182\) voxels and was distributed across both hemispheres (Figure~\ref{fig:main_vigour_network}), and was not limited to a single motor cluster. The largest portions of the map were in temporal, parietal, and frontal cortices. A sensorimotor component was also present in precentral, postcentral, paracentral, and supplementary motor areas. Additional clusters extended into the orbitofrontal cortex, occipital cortex, fusiform and parahippocampal regions, hippocampus, amygdala, caudate, putamen, thalamus, and cerebellum (Figure~\ref{fig:main_vigour_network} and Figure~S8 of the Supplementary Material).

Overall, the optimised map reflected a distributed network that included motor‑related regions but extended well beyond primary movement‑related areas, with strong coverage in temporal, parietal, frontal, limbic, subcortical, and cerebellar regions.

\subsubsection{The vigour network was distinct from standard task activation}
We compared the vigour network with the task-activation map to test whether the vigour network was mainly a subset of the task-activation map (Figures~\ref{fig:main_network_task_overlap} and \ref{fig:roi_task_overlap_summary}). The two maps showed limited same-voxel overlap. The vigour network contained \(8{,}182\) voxels, whereas the task-activation map contained \(21{,}610\) voxels. Only \(835\) voxels were shared, giving a Dice coefficient of \(0.056\) and a Jaccard index of \(0.029\).

The task‑activation map broadly covered regions engaged during task performance, whereas the vigour network was more selective. Some overlap occurred in motor‑related regions—including pericentral cortex, supplementary/paracentral motor areas, putamen, cerebellum, and parietal cortex—but many regions contained both maps with little or no same‑voxel overlap. Temporal‑limbic and orbitofrontal components were more prominent in the vigour network than in the task‑activation map (Figure~\ref{fig:roi_task_overlap_summary}; Tables~S4 and S5 of the Supplementary Material).

\subsection{Stage 2: Stimulation and medication sensitivity of the vigour network}
After validating the network, we tested whether its functional organisation changed with dopaminergic medication and with different GVS stimuli.

\subsubsection{GVS-specific modulation of behaviour and network responses}
Across the eight GVS waveforms, the behavioural effect of GVS depended on the medication state. In the OFF-medication session, GVS7 was the only GVS waveform that differed significantly from sham after correction for multiple comparisons, producing faster responses, reflected by lower normalised RTs (\(p = 0.004\), FDR \(q = 0.033\); Figure~\ref{fig:gvs_behaviour_network_responses} first row). No GVS waveform survived correction in the ON-medication session.

Projected time courses suggested stimulus‑specific modulation of the vigour‑network signal. Across GVS conditions, the vigour‑network projection showed distinct waveform‑ and medication‑dependent patterns (Figure~S11A of the Supplementary Material). For the \textit{late-early response} feature, GVS7 was the only GVS waveform that differed from sham in the OFF-medication session (Figure~\ref{fig:gvs_behaviour_network_responses} middle row). The negative active-minus-sham difference indicated that GVS7 reduced the late relative to the early component of the projected vigour network response. This effect was absent during the ON-medication session, in which no GVS waveform survived correction. The full vigour network feature summaries appear in Figure~S12A, B of the Supplementary Material.

In contrast, the task-activation map projection did not mirror this behavioural specificity.  In the ON-medication session, no task-activation map response survived correction, similar to the vigour network. However, in the OFF-medication session, significant task-activation map effects appeared for other stimulation conditions (e.g., GVS2 and GVS8 for the late–early feature; GVS6 for the maximum absolute baseline‑response feature (Figure~\ref{fig:gvs_behaviour_network_responses} bottom row; Figures~S11B and S13A, B of the Supplementary Material). Thus, the stimulation condition associated with faster OFF-medication responses was reflected in the vigour-network projection, but not by the task-activation projection.

We next tested whether the vigour network showed stimulation-sensitive connectivity changes distinct from those seen in the task-activation map. Because participants contributed multiple GVS conditions, observations were not fully independent; therefore, these analyses describe stimulation-sensitive edges rather than a causal therapeutic GVS effect. Using mutual information, active GVS produced \(156\) sham-referenced edge changes that were significant after FDR correction among \(703\) tested edges (Figure~\ref{fig:gvs_edge_changes}). Using Pearson correlation, active GVS produced \(194\) significant edges (Figure~S14A of the Supplementary Material). The two metrics did not identify the same set of edges, consistent with the different dependencies captured by the two metrics. As a control, we repeated the GVS edge-change analysis using the task-activation map (Figures~S14B and S15 of the Supplementary Material). GVS also produced significant changes in this task-activation network, but the pattern differed: the task-activation map showed \(134\) significant mutual-information edges and \(148\) significant Pearson edges, with only \(14\) mutual-information edges and \(16\) Pearson edges overlapping those in the vigour network. This supports a cautious interpretation: GVS influenced task-active circuitry broadly, but the vigour network showed a partly distinct pattern of stimulation-sensitive connectivity. Detailed edge-change results are reported in the Supplementary Material.

\subsubsection{Medication strengthened local coupling within the vigour network}
We next tested whether dopaminergic medication altered vigour-network connectivity. Network-pattern dissimilarity analysis showed that connectivity patterns differed more between medication states than between repeated runs within the same state, for both Pearson- and mutual-information-based edges (Figure~S9 of the Supplementary Material).

We then assessed whether medication-related reorganisation occurred locally within ROIs or between ROIs (Figure~\ref{fig:medication_local_distributed_fc}). Medication was mainly associated with increased local connectivity within vigour-network ROIs. Within-ROI FC increased significantly from OFF to ON-medication (\(\Delta_{\text{ON–OFF}}=+0.0147\), \(p=0.006\)), whereas between-ROI FC showed no reliable change and remained close to zero (\(\Delta_{\text{ON–OFF}}=-0.0006\), \(p=0.723\)). This suggested that dopaminergic medication strengthened local functional coupling within the vigour network rather than broadly increasing distributed connectivity between ROIs.

To assess whether this pattern was specific to the vigour network, we repeated the same analysis using the task-activation map. The task-activation map also showed a significant difference between within-ROI and between-ROI medication-related changes (\(p=0.012\); Figure~\ref{fig:medication_local_distributed_fc}). However, the underlying pattern was weaker and less specific than in the vigour network. In the vigour network, the effect was driven by a significant increase in within-ROI FC. In the task-activation map, within-ROI FC showed only a non-significant increase (\(\Delta_{\text{ON–OFF}}=+0.0081\), \(p=0.108\)), together with a non-significant tendency toward reduced between-ROI FC (\(\Delta_{\text{ON–OFF}}=-0.0098\), \(p=0.142\)). Thus, while task-active regions also exhibited a local-versus-distributed medication contrast, the vigour network more clearly captured medication-related strengthening of local coupling.

\section{Discussion}
This study investigated whether trial-wise variability in motor vigour in PD reflects instability in a central vigour-related network, or whether some behavioural variability arises downstream from a relatively stable original motor command. The results are more consistent with the latter interpretation. Using fMRI during a ballistic hand-squeeze task, we identified a distributed vigour network that tracked RT while showing lower consecutive-trial variability than the behavioural signal itself. The main finding is therefore not simply that RT was associated with brain activity. Rather, our results suggest a behaviour-linked neural expression of motor vigour in PD that was more stable than the behaviour. The vigour network included a motor-related core with contributions from pericentral cortex, superior parietal cortex, putamen, and cerebellar regions. This is consistent with prior work implicating motor cortical, basal-ganglia, thalamic, and cerebellar systems in movement speed, force, amplitude, and vigour \citep{anderson2012ecog,dudman2016basal,shirinbayan2019cortical,spraker2007basalganglia,turner2010basal,yttri2016opponent,niv2007tonic,zenon2016dopamine}. Amygdala involvement is also plausible because primate amygdala neurons encode positive and negative visual-stimulus value during learning \citep{paton2006primate}, and anatomical evidence supports an amygdala-motor pathway through which affective or salient cues can shape action \citep{grezes2014direct}. Hippocampal and parahippocampal involvement may reflect contextual organisation of action: reward behaviour depends on hippocampus-nucleus accumbens synaptic strength \citep{legates2018reward}, the hippocampus can organise upcoming skilled action sequences \citep{yewbrey2024hippocampus}, and hippocampal mechanisms support visually cued contextual response selection \citep{kim2012hippocampus}. Thus, the vigour network included regions that can integrate cue value, context, salience, motivational state, and action readiness to determine whether an action is initiated quickly on a given trial \citep{wallis2007orbitofrontal,rolls2020orbitofrontal,pierrieau2025changes}.



The present findings are difficult to reconcile with a simple interpretation in which variable motor vigour solely reflects an unstable central vigour-related network. Instead, they suggest that a relatively stable behaviour-linked projection can be recovered in PD, and that some behavioural fluctuation in movement initiation may arise as this command is converted into movement. Importantly, this does not mean that lower BOLD variability is always better: for example, in expert racing drivers, higher BOLD temporal variability has been interpreted as part of a more efficient task-related architecture, whereas in an adult lifespan working-memory sample, higher BOLD variability was linked to poorer performance \citep{grady2014understanding,boylan2021greater}. The direction of the effect, therefore, has to be interpreted in relation to the task, population, and neural system being studied.


The GVS analyses suggested that the vigour network was sensitive to external vestibular perturbation, but not in a general or uniform manner. Among the GVS waveforms, only GVS7 (125 Hz carrier and 16 Hz envelope) produced a corrected behavioural effect, with faster responses in the OFF-medication session. Importantly, this behaviourally effective waveform was also the only GVS waveform that altered the late-early feature of the vigour network projection in the same medication state. This pattern was not reproduced by the task-activation network: significant task-activation map effects appeared for other stimulation conditions rather than for GVS7. Thus, the key finding was not simply that GVS altered both behaviour and brain activity, but that the only stimulation waveform associated with improved behavioural vigour was also selectively reflected in the vigour-related projection. This supports the interpretation that the vigour network captured behaviourally relevant stimulation effects more specifically than the broader task-activation network. Relative to sham stimulation, active GVS produced connectivity changes within the vigour network, but the direction and anatomical distribution of these changes were heterogeneous. The task-activation map also showed GVS-related edge changes, but with limited overlap with the vigour network pattern. These findings suggest that GVS broadly changed task-related circuitry, while revealing a partly distinct set of stimulation-sensitive interactions within the vigour network. However, because the edge analyses pooled repeated block-level observations across participants, medication states, runs, and stimulation conditions, they should be interpreted as evidence of network perturbability rather than of a specific therapeutic mechanism.


The medication analysis showed that the vigour network was not fixed across the dopaminergic state. Connectivity patterns within the network differed more between OFF and ON-medication than between repeated runs within the same medication state, for both Pearson-based and mutual-information-based measures. More specifically, ON-medication was associated with a selective increase in local within-ROI coupling, whereas between-ROI coupling remained close to zero and did not change reliably. This pattern was clearer in the vigour network than in the task-activation map: it showed a significant local-versus-distributed contrast, but its separate within-ROI and between-ROI changes were weaker and individually non-significant. Thus, the main medication-related finding was not a global increase in network connectivity, nor was there evidence that medication normalised the network. Rather, a dopaminergic state was associated with stronger local coherence within the vigour network. This interpretation is consistent with prior work linking dopamine to motor vigour and with evidence that levodopa can alter functional network organisation in PD \citep{niv2007tonic,berman2016levodopa,zenon2016dopamine,mazzoni2007parkinson}.


This study has several limitations. We included only participants with PD, so we cannot say whether the vigour network would also be observed more generally in healthy controls. Motor vigour was indexed mainly by reaction time, which captures response initiation; however, other components such as movement speed, force amplitude, force rise time, and movement duration are also important aspects of motor vigour and may dissociate depending on task demands \citep{pierrieau2025changes}. The fixed OFF-then-ON also limits causal interpretation of medication order (necessitated for safety and comfort of the participants), which may confound medication effects with practice, fatigue, time-on-task, or scanner drift. In addition, reward cues, GVS stimulus, block context, and adaptation across the run may have contributed to both RT and trial-wise neural activity, not just the vigour network. A related methodological caveat is that BOLD variability can include non-neural vascular contributions; scaling with resting-state fluctuation amplitude or breath-hold responses can reduce this variability, especially in block-design motor fMRI \citep{kannurpatti2011non}. Finally, the optimisation was linear and was developed and characterised in the same dataset. Cross-validation and control analyses reduce this concern, but independent validation is needed in new PD cohorts, healthy adults, and different motor tasks. An argument could be made that our results are the result of a circular argument: low consecutive-trial variability was one optimisation criterion. However, the model was constrained not only by stability, but also by task engagement, behavioural relevance, and spatial coherence. At the subject level, the optimised projection was less variable than RT. At the voxel level, the selected voxels were concentrated among low-variability voxels and showed lower mean variability than size-matched samples from non-selected motor-area voxels. These validation analyses support the view that the solution was neither an arbitrary smooth sample of motor activity nor simply a restatement of behavioural variability.

Overall, the present study suggested that trial-wise vigour variability in PD should not be interpreted simply as instability of a central vigour-related command. A relatively stable, behaviour-linked neural activity could be recovered, and this activity was organised across motor, striatal, limbic, contextual, and motivational systems. Behavioural variability may therefore partly reflect how central vigour-related coding is converted into movement, and dopaminergic state and vestibular stimulation also modulated the upstream network through which this coding is expressed.

\section{Conclusions}
We identified a distributed vigour-related neural projection in Parkinson's disease whose trial-wise expression was more stable than the behavioural reaction-time fluctuations it tracked. This projection was anatomically distributed, differed from standard task activation, and showed dopaminergic-state-related strengthening of local within-ROI coupling. GVS analyses further suggested that one OFF-medication stimulation waveform produced a behavioural effect mirrored by the vigour network projection, while broader connectivity analyses revealed heterogeneous stimulation-dependent perturbations. Together, the findings support a model in which PD-related variability in response initiation is not solely the product of an unstable central command. Instead, a relatively stable central vigour-related network can be recovered, while behavioural variability may partly reflect how that projection is translated into motor output under different dopaminergic and stimulation states. This vigour network could provide a target for non-invasive brain stimulation to mitigate some of the motor effects of PD.

\section*{Appendix A: Optimisation derivation and model-selection summary}
The optimisation framework identifies a voxel-weight vector and a projected signal related to trial-wise motor vigour while satisfying constraints on task engagement, spatial coherence, and low consecutive-trial variability. In voxel space, the projected signals \( \mathbf{y}_{\beta} \in \mathbb{R}^{n_{\mathrm{trials}}} \) and \( \mathbf{y}_{\mathrm{BOLD}} \in \mathbb{R}^{n_{\mathrm{time}}} \) are defined as:
\begin{equation*}\mathbf{y}_{\beta} = \mathbf{w}^{\top}\mathbf{X}_{\beta} \qquad \mathbf{y}_{\mathrm{BOLD}} = \mathbf{w}^{\top}\mathbf{X}_{\mathrm{BOLD}}\end{equation*}
\( \mathbf{X}_{\beta} \in \mathbb{R}^{n_{\mathrm{voxels}} \times n_{\mathrm{trials}}} \) contains the single-trial beta estimates, \( \mathbf{X}_{\mathrm{BOLD}} \in \mathbb{R}^{n_{\mathrm{voxels}} \times n_{\mathrm{time}}} \) contains the BOLD time courses, and \( \mathbf{w} \in \mathbb{R}^{n_{\mathrm{voxels}}} \) denotes the model weights. The optimisation model was based on four constraints. First, the signal should come mainly from task-engaged voxels. This term discourages the model from assigning large weights to weakly task-engaged components. For voxel \(v\), let \(a_v=\|X_{\beta,v:}\|_2\), \(\mathbf{a}=(a_1,\ldots, a_{n_{\mathrm{voxels}}})^\top \)
where \(X_{\beta,v:}\) is the trial-wise beta for voxel \(v\). The task-engagement penalty was 
\[
L_{\mathrm{task}}(w)=w^\top C_{\mathrm{task}}w,\qquad
C_{\mathrm{task}}=
\left[\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{a}+\epsilon\mathbf{1})\right]^{-1},
\]
where \(\epsilon>0\) prevents division by zero.
% \begin{equation}
% L_{\mathrm{task}}(w)
% =
% w^\top C_{\mathrm{task}}w,
% \qquad
% C_{\mathrm{task}}
% =
% \operatorname{diag}\!\left(\left|X_{\beta}\right|\right)^{-1}
% \end{equation}
Second, the projected beta signal should encode behavioural information. The behavioural variable was reaction time. Therefore, the model aimed to maximise the correlation between \(X_{\mathrm{behav}}\) (RT) and \(y_{\beta}\):
\begin{equation*}
\max_w
\operatorname{Corr}\!\left(X_{\mathrm{behav}}, w^\top X_{\beta}\right)^2
=
\frac{w^\top X_{\beta}X_{\mathrm{behav}}^\top X_{\mathrm{behav}}X_{\beta}^\top w}{w^\top X_{\beta}X_{\beta}^\top  w}
=
\frac{w^\top C_{\mathrm{RT}} w}{w^\top C_{\mathrm{var}} w}
\end{equation*}
Third, the selected voxels should form a spatially coherent network. Neighbouring voxels were encouraged to have similar weights using the spatial smoothness loss: \(L_{\mathrm{smooth}}(w) = w^\top C_{\mathrm{smooth}}w\), \(C_{\mathrm{smooth}} = L_{\mathrm{graph}}\).
% Here, \(C_{RT}\) captures the behaviourally relevant part of the projected beta signal, and \(C_{var}\) normalises this association by the variance of the projected beta signal.

Fourth, the projected signal should show low consecutive-trial variability. This condition penalised large local changes between neighbouring trials, rather than variance across the full time. For valid consecutive-trial pairs \(I_c\), the beta estimate variability loss was
\begin{equation*} 
L_{\beta}(w) = w^\top C_{\beta}w, 
\qquad
C_{\beta} = \sum_{i\in I_c} \left(X_{\beta,i}-X_{\beta,i+1}\right) \left(X_{\beta,i}-X_{\beta,i+1}\right)^\top 
\end{equation*}
The same low-variability condition was also applied to BOLD: \(L_{BOLD}(w) = w^\top C_{BOLD}w\). Both variability terms were included because \(L_{\beta}(w)\) constrains variability in movement-related beta responses, whereas \(L_{\mathrm{bold}}(w)\) also captures broader task-related variability present in the BOLD.

The four penalty terms were combined into a single total loss:
\begin{equation*}
\begin{split}
L_{\mathrm{total}}(w)
&=
\alpha_{\mathrm{task}} L_{\mathrm{task}}(w)
+
\alpha_{\mathrm{BOLD}} L_{\mathrm{bold}}(w)
+
\alpha_{\beta} L_{\beta}(w)
+
\alpha_{\mathrm{smooth}} L_{\mathrm{smooth}}(w)
=
w^\top C_{\mathrm{total}}w \\
C_{\mathrm{total}}
&=
\alpha_{\mathrm{task}} C_{\mathrm{task}}
+
\alpha_{\mathrm{BOLD}} C_{\mathrm{bold}}
+
\alpha_{\beta} C_{\beta}
+
\alpha_{\mathrm{smooth}} C_{\mathrm{smooth}}
\end{split}
\end{equation*}
The final objective minimises the combined penalties while maximising behavioural relevance:
\begin{equation*}
\min_w
\frac{
\gamma w^\top C_{\mathrm{total}}w
-
w^\top C_{RT}w
}{
w^\top C_{var}w
}
\qquad
\mathrm{s.t.}\ \|w\|_2=1
\end{equation*}
Here, \(\alpha_{\mathrm{task}}\), \(\alpha_{\mathrm{BOLD}}\), \(\alpha_{\beta}\), and \(\alpha_{\mathrm{smooth}}\) control the relative contribution of each penalty term, and \(\gamma\) controls the overall strength of the penalties relative to behavioural relevance. 

The optimisation was solved with Sequential Least Squares Programming, and hyperparameters were selected by coarse-to-fine cross-validation. Candidate settings were fitted on training folds and evaluated using held-out \(|\operatorname{Corr}(y_{\beta}, RT)|\), favouring stable performance and small train-test differences. 
Since voxel-space optimisation was computationally impractical at this dimensionality, the data were first projected into a lower-dimensional PCA subspace.
