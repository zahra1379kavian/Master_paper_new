\title{Beyond Task Activation: A Consistent vigour-Related Network in Parkinson’s Disease}

\author[\firstAuthorLast ]{\Authors}
\address{}
\correspondance{}
\extraAuth{}

\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
\section{}
Movement vigour varies from trial to trial in Parkinson's disease (PD), but behaviour alone cannot reveal whether this variability reflects an unstable central vigour-related signal or later noise in translating that signal into movement. We analysed trial-wise fMRI and reaction time from \(18\) participants with PD performing a reward-cued ballistic hand-squeeze task in OFF- and ON-medication sessions, with galvanic vestibular stimulation (GVS) delivered in randomized blocks. Single-trial neural responses were estimated with GLMsingle and entered into a constrained optimization framework that learned a whole-brain voxel-weight map. The model selected weights that favoured task engagement, reaction-time relevance, spatial coherence, and low consecutive-trial variability. This optimized voxel-weight map defined a vigour network, and its trial-wise weighted projected signal was used to test whether the network carried a stable behaviour-linked signal. The vigour network tracked reaction time but varied less abruptly than behaviour, supporting the presence of a relatively stable central vigour-related component in PD. Anatomically, the vigour network included motor, striatal, cerebellar, thalamic, parietal, temporal-limbic, and orbitofrontal regions, and was largely distinct from a conventional task-activation map. Dopaminergic state was associated with stronger local coupling within the network, rather than a broad increase in connectivity between regions. Exploratory GVS analyses showed heterogeneous connectivity changes within the network. These findings suggest that a relatively stable central vigour-related signal can be recovered in PD, while behavioural variability may arise partly during the state-dependent translation of that signal into movement.

\section{Introduction}
Movement vigour refers to how quickly and energetically an action is initiated and carried out. It is not a fixed property of the movement: the same person can perform the same action faster or slower across trials, and different people often express different levels of speed and force during the same task \citep{berret2018vigour}. Human fMRI work links reach speed to a distributed set of cortical and subcortical regions, including motor and premotor cortices, supplementary motor area, basal ganglia, thalamus, parietal cortex, and cerebellum \citep{shirinbayan2019cortical}. ECoG and basal-ganglia studies further support movement-related cortical activity and subcortical contributions to force amplitude \citep{anderson2012ecog,spraker2007basalganglia}. These findings suggest that vigour is not merely a behavioural output, but a regulated feature of action controlled by interacting neural circuits.

Dopamine is central to this regulation. Basal-ganglia output can change movement speed and amplitude without necessarily changing which action is selected \citep{turner2010basal}, and striatal pathway manipulations can speed up or slow down movement in a dopamine-dependent manner \citep{yttri2016opponent}. Reward and motivational value also influence vigour: actions associated with higher value are often initiated faster and executed with greater speed \citep{kawagoe1998expectation,reppert2015modulation,summerside2018vigor}. Parkinson's disease (PD), which is characterized by dopaminergic loss in basal-ganglia circuits, is therefore a useful model for asking how dopaminergic state shapes the consistency of vigour expression.

A core motor symptom of PD is bradykinesia, usually described as slower and smaller movements. The problem, however, is not simply that patients cannot move quickly. When speed is strongly encouraged, patients with PD can sometimes produce rapid movements \citep{mazzoni2007parkinson}. This suggests that PD may disrupt how vigour is regulated and deployed from one trial to the next. Behavioural studies report larger short-term response-time fluctuations in PD \citep{macdonald2025capturing}, and earlier work found increased endpoint variability that could be partly reduced when participants slowed down or relied on visual guidance \citep{sheridan1990bradykinesia}. Motor output is also less stable: motor-unit discharge can become more variable in mild-to-moderate PD even when mean discharge rates are relatively preserved \citep{wilson2020motor}. Dopamine loss may therefore affect both average movement features and the consistency with which those features are expressed \citep{lee2015dopamine}.

The key difficulty is that behavioural variability has several possible sources. Some movement variability is useful, helping the motor system explore actions, adapt after errors, and learn from reward \citep{pekny2015reward,wu2014variability}. Some variability reflects the neural state before movement; preparatory activity in motor and premotor cortex can predict the speed of the upcoming reach \citep{churchland2006preparatory}. Another source is motor noise \citep{faisal2008noise}, especially signal-dependent noise, where larger motor commands generate greater variability and make fast or forceful movements harder to control accurately \citep{harris1998signal}. In PD, such downstream noise may be behaviourally costly: signal-dependent noise has been linked to effort costs in the more affected arm, and reducing this noise increased patients' willingness to use that arm \citep{salimpour2015altering}. High motor noise can also weaken the link between an action and its outcome, impairing learning \citep{therrien2016cerebellar}.

Because these sources occur together, behaviour alone cannot reveal whether variable vigour reflects instability of a central neural signal or noise added later in the motor pathway. This distinction matters for PD. If the central vigour signal itself is unstable, then bradykinesia and inconsistency may reflect impaired neural coding. If a relatively stable central signal still tracks trial-wise behaviour while behavioural output fluctuates, then part of the instability may arise downstream of that signal or during its state-dependent translation into movement. Trial-wise fMRI provides a way to test this idea because it can estimate neural responses for individual trials and relate those responses to moment-to-moment behaviour rather than only to average task activation \citep{dinstein2015neural,garrett2013moment,haar2017individual}.

The present study was designed for this central-versus-downstream question while also testing whether the recovered network is sensitive to dopaminergic and vestibular modulation. We analysed a reward-cued ballistic hand-squeeze task in PD participants scanned OFF and ON dopaminergic medication. During the task, participants also received block-wise galvanic vestibular stimulation (GVS), an exploratory perturbation relevant to motor-control and movement-disorder stimulation research. We developed a constrained optimization framework to recover a neural projection that was task-engaged, linked to behaviour, spatially coherent, and low in consecutive-trial variability. We then asked four questions: (i) whether the projection was reproducible and less variable than behaviour, (ii) whether its anatomical distribution was distinct from standard task activation, (iii) whether dopaminergic medication changed its network organization, and (iv) whether GVS perturbed connectivity within the recovered network.

\section{Materials and Methods}

\subsection{Participants and medication-state design}
Eighteen participants with Parkinson's disease were included in the final analysis (\(4\) female, \(14\) male; mean age \(67.22 \pm 7.03\) years, range \(55\)-\(82\)). Fourteen participants were right-handed, and four were left-handed. Participants performed the task with their dominant hand, except for one right-handed participant who used the left hand because tremor in the dominant hand was severe. Data from a subject, session 1 (OFF medication), were excluded because one recording run was excessively noisy. Exclusion criteria included prior neurosurgical treatment for PD, other neurological or major medical conditions, dementia severe enough to preclude informed consent, severe levodopa-induced dyskinesia incompatible with MRI, and standard contraindications to MRI. Clinical severity was assessed using the MDS-UPDRS Part III \citep{goetz2008movement}; detailed medication-state and domain summaries are reported in Supplementary Tables~S1 and S2. All participants provided written informed consent, and the study was approved by the Clinical Research Ethics Board at the University of British Columbia.

Participants completed two medication sessions on the same day: OFF medication and ON medication. They were instructed to stop dopaminergic medication at least \(12\)~h before recording. The session order was fixed across participants: the OFF-medication session was always completed first, after which participants took their dopaminergic medication and completed the ON-medication session approximately \(1\)~h later. Because OFF sessions always preceded ON sessions, medication effects could be partly confounded with practice, fatigue, time-on-task, or scanner drift. 

\subsection{Behavioural task and GVS protocol}
During fMRI, participants performed a ballistic hand-squeeze task implemented in MATLAB with Psychtoolbox \citep{brainard1997psychophysics} while lying supine in the scanner and viewing the task through a mirror display (Figure~\ref{fig:task_analysis_overview}A). On each trial, a visual cue indicating reward value was presented for \(2\)~s, followed by a variable delay of \(2\)-\(3.5\)~s. A moving balloon then appeared as the Go signal. Participants were instructed to squeeze a mechanical bulb as quickly and forcefully as possible when the Go signal appeared, and successful responses were followed by a visual burst of the balloon. Reaction time (RT), defined as the interval between Go-signal onset and squeeze initiation, was used as the primary trial-wise index of motor vigour because it was the most robust behavioural measure in this dataset.

Each medication session consisted of two task runs, and each run included nine blocks of \(10\) trials. Most trials were rewarded, with low- and high-reward conditions counterbalanced across runs, and \(10\%\) were catch trials in which participants were required to withhold their response to avoid a penalty. Task difficulty was adjusted online by varying the response window based on recent hit rate, with the aim of maintaining accuracy at or above \(65\%\). A \(20\)~s rest period was provided after every three blocks. Behavioural quality-control and reward-related RT summaries are reported in Supplementary Figures~S1-S3.

GVS was delivered continuously throughout each run using bilateral mastoid electrodes. Each run contained one sham block and eight active amplitude-modulated GVS blocks. The active waveforms crossed carrier frequency (\(75\) or \(125\)~Hz) with envelope frequency (\(0\), \(10\), \(16\), or \(20\)~Hz), producing GVS1--GVS8. The order of the nine stimulation conditions was randomized independently for each run. The GVS analyses were considered exploratory because stimulation was presented in short blocks, observations from the same participant were repeated, and no dedicated washout interval separated every adjacent block. Full stimulation parameters, electrode placement, thresholding, and block-order details are provided in Supplementary Methods.

\subsection{MRI acquisition, preprocessing, and trial-wise beta estimation}
Structural and functional MRI data were acquired on a Philips \(3\)T Ingenia Elition X whole-body MRI scanner upgraded to an MR7700 system (Philips Healthcare, Best, The Netherlands) using a \(32\)-channel head coil. For each participant, a T1-weighted structural image was acquired, followed by two task-based fMRI scans per medication session. Functional MRI was acquired using EPI. The first three participants were scanned with a multi-echo EPI protocol, whereas the remaining participants were scanned with a single-echo EPI protocol. This protocol difference is reported explicitly because it may have affected signal-to-noise, temporal sampling, or trial-wise beta estimation. Full sequence parameters are provided in Supplementary Methods.

Image preprocessing was performed in FSL \citep{smith2004advances}. Structural images underwent bias-field correction and brain extraction. Functional images were corrected for head motion and susceptibility-induced distortion, high-pass filtered, brain extracted, denoised using ICA-AROMA \citep{pruim2015ica}, spatially smoothed with a \(5\)~mm full-width at half-maximum Gaussian kernel, registered to each participant's structural image, and normalized to MNI152 space. The scanner automatically discarded four dummy volumes before saved image acquisition, so no additional initial volumes were removed during preprocessing.

Task-related responses for individual trials were estimated using GLMsingle \citep{kay2013glmdenoise,prince2022improving}. Analyses were performed separately for each subject-session, with the two task runs analysed jointly while preserving run identity in the design matrix. Go-signal onsets were modeled using \(2\)-s event regressors; catch trials were excluded because no squeeze response was required. The mean CSF time series was included as a nuisance regressor. GLMsingle was run with HRF fitting, GLMdenoise, and fractional ridge regression enabled. After robust beta-value cleaning and group-level coverage filtering, the final group beta matrix contained \(91{,}769\) voxels and \(5{,}463\) retained Go trials. Detailed beta-cleaning thresholds, outlier rules, and coverage calculations are reported in Supplementary Methods.

\subsection{Standard task-activation reference map}
To provide a conventional task-engagement reference, we ran a standard FSL FEAT GLM separate from the trial-wise GLMsingle model. All valid Go trials were modeled with one event regressor defined at the Go-signal onset, with duration \(2\)~s and amplitude \(1\). First-level maps tested the positive effect of task activity, runs were combined within subject-session using fixed effects, and subject-session maps were entered into a group mixed-effects model using FLAME stage~1 \citep{woolrich2004multilevel,beckmann2003general}. Group maps were thresholded using clusters formed at \(Z \geq 3.1\) with corrected cluster significance \(p \leq 0.05\) \citep{worsley200114}. This thresholded map was used only as a standard task-activation reference, not as the main model. The full standard GLM map and comparisons with GLMsingle activation maps are shown in Supplementary Figures~S4 and S5.

\subsection{Constrained optimization framework}
We used a group-level optimization procedure to identify a vigour-related brain network (Figure~\ref{fig:task_analysis_overview}B,C). The purpose was to find a voxel-weight vector that combined whole-brain activity into one scalar neural projection per trial. For the trial-wise beta matrix \(X_{\beta}\), the projected signal was
\begin{equation}
y = w^\top X_{\beta},
\end{equation}
where \(w\) is the weight vector and \(y\) is the projected trial-wise neural signal. The same projection framework was also applied to trial-window BOLD features to constrain local trial-to-trial instability. Because direct optimization over all voxels was computationally impractical, beta and BOLD data were reduced with expectation-maximization principal component analysis (EMPCA) \citep{roweis1997algorithms}. An \(80\%\) cumulative explained-variance threshold retained \(14\) components for optimization; final weights were projected back to voxel space after model fitting.

The model encoded four assumptions. First, the selected signal should come from voxels engaged by the task. Second, the projected beta signal should be behaviourally relevant to RT. Third, neighbouring voxels should have similar weights, producing a spatially coherent map rather than isolated scattered voxels. Fourth, the projected signal should show low consecutive-trial variability. Consecutive-trial variability was used rather than total variance because it preserves trial order and directly penalizes abrupt local jumps while allowing slower task-related changes across runs or blocks. Run and block boundaries were excluded from this penalty because they do not represent continuous local temporal dynamics.

The optimization combined the task-engagement, BOLD variability, beta variability, and spatial-smoothness penalties into
\begin{equation}
C_{\mathrm{total}}
= \alpha_{\mathrm{task}} C_{\mathrm{task}}
+ \alpha_{\mathrm{BOLD}} C_{\mathrm{BOLD}}
+ \alpha_{\beta} C_{\beta}
+ \alpha_{\mathrm{smooth}} C_{\mathrm{smooth}} .
\end{equation}
Behavioural relevance was represented by a correlation-based term linking the projected beta signal to RT. The final objective minimized penalties while maximizing behavioural relevance:
\begin{equation}
\min_w
\frac{\gamma w^\top C_{\mathrm{total}}w - w^\top C_b w}{w^\top C_d w}
\quad
\mathrm{s.t.}\ \|w\|_2=1 .
\end{equation}
Here, \(C_b\) captures alignment between the projected beta signal and RT, \(C_d\) normalizes by the projected beta variance, the \(\alpha\) values control the four penalty weights, and \(\gamma\) controls the overall balance between regularization and behavioural relevance. Full definitions of all terms and derivations are provided in Supplementary Methods.

The constrained optimization was solved using Sequential Least Squares Programming (SLSQP) \citep{nocedal2006numerical,kraft1988software}. Hyperparameters were selected with a coarse-to-fine cross-validation procedure. In the coarse stage, candidate penalty-weight combinations were evaluated over contiguous folds using held-out absolute correlation between the optimized neural projection and behaviour. After the best coarse setting was identified, a fine search tested multiplicative perturbations around that setting. The final setting was chosen based on held-out correlation, stability across folds, and small train-test differences. For each fold, the model was fit on training data, weights were projected back into voxel space, and final voxel maps and projected signals were obtained by averaging across folds.

\subsection{Network definition and validation}
The final vigour network was defined as voxels with weights above the \(90\)th percentile. This threshold was chosen because it provided a stable compromise between spatial specificity and anatomical coverage: lower thresholds retained substantially more voxels, whereas stricter thresholds began to remove several anatomical groups. Threshold robustness is shown in Supplementary Figure~S6.

We validated the solution in three ways. First, we assessed cross-validation reproducibility of the optimized voxel-weight maps using intraclass correlation (ICC). Second, we compared the consecutive-trial variability of the projected neural signal with the consecutive-trial variability of RT. The normalized consecutive-trial variability metric was
\begin{equation}
\sum_i \frac{(x_i-x_{i+1})^2}{x_i^2+x_{i+1}^2},
\end{equation}
computed after excluding run and block boundaries. Inferential statistics used subject-level summaries. Third, we tested whether selected voxels were less variable than a motor-area control pool. The control pool was defined from bilateral motor-area atlas labels after removing selected voxels, and \(1{,}000\) size-matched samples were drawn without replacement.

\subsection{Medication-state network analysis}
After defining the vigour network, we tested whether dopaminergic state altered its functional organization. Medication state was not used during network optimization, so this analysis provided an independent characterization of the final network. Only participants with data in both medication states were included in paired analyses (\(n=17\)). Selected voxels were assigned to atlas-defined ROIs, and the optimized voxel weights were used to compute a weighted mean beta series for each ROI. The same analysis was repeated using the standard task-activation map as a reference network.

We used two complementary undirected functional connectivity metrics: Pearson correlation, which captures linear co-fluctuation, and mutual information, which captures more general statistical dependence. Mutual information was estimated after discretizing each beta series into three rank-based quantile bins. Because both metrics are symmetric, related effects were interpreted as changes in statistical coupling, not causal influence.

The main medication analysis tested whether medication preferentially affected local within-ROI coupling or distributed between-ROI coupling. Between-region connectivity was the average Fisher-\(z\)-transformed Pearson correlation between all pairs of ROI beta series. Within-region connectivity was the average Fisher-\(z\)-transformed Pearson correlation between voxel beta series inside each ROI; ROI-level within-region values were averaged equally across ROIs so that larger ROIs did not dominate. The primary contrast tested whether the ON-OFF change in within-ROI connectivity was larger than the ON-OFF change in between-ROI connectivity.

\subsection{GVS-related network analysis}
We next examined whether GVS modulated functional connectivity within the same ROI sets. For each subject, medication state, run, and GVS condition, trial-wise ROI beta estimates were grouped by stimulation block. The sham block served as the within-run baseline, and each active GVS condition was compared with the matched sham block from the same run.

Connectivity was computed using the same two edge metrics defined above: Pearson correlation and mutual information. For each active GVS block, active-minus-sham edge changes were computed separately for each metric. Thus, the GVS analysis asked whether stimulation changed ROI-to-ROI statistical coupling relative to the matched sham condition. Block-level edge-change values were pooled across subjects, medication states, runs, and active GVS conditions. Each edge was tested against zero using a two-sided sign-flip permutation test with \(5{,}000\) permutations, followed by false-discovery-rate (FDR) correction within metric and analysis scope. Because this analysis included repeated block-level observations from participants and each block contained few trials, GVS findings were treated as exploratory.

\section{Results}
We first tested whether the optimization recovered a behaviour-linked signal with low consecutive-trial variability. We then characterized the anatomy of the resulting network, compared it with standard task activation, and examined medication- and GVS-related network effects.

\subsection{Behavioural performance}
After trial- and run-level quality control, \(18\) participants, \(35\) sessions, \(70\) runs, and \(5{,}463\) Go trials were retained for behavioural and neural analyses. Mean RT was \(387.2 \pm 65.8\)~ms in the OFF-medication state and \(375.5 \pm 64.7\)~ms in the ON-medication state. Consecutive-trial RT variability was \(3.50\) in the OFF state and \(4.04\) in the ON state. The subject-paired medication effect on mean RT was small (Supplementary Figure~S2). Reward-condition effects were heterogeneous across subjects, and GVS produced no consistent group-level RT effect (Supplementary Figure~S3).

\subsection{Optimization performance: identifies a behaviour-linked low-variability signal}
The optimized voxel-weight maps were highly consistent across cross-validation folds (ICC \(=0.978\)), indicating that the solution was not driven by a single fold. The resulting projection, \(y\), was a one-dimensional weighted summary of voxel activity that jointly reflected task engagement, behavioural relevance, spatial coherence, and low consecutive-trial variability. We then asked whether this projected neural signal was less variable than the behaviour it was designed to track. Using subject-level comparison, behaviour-minus-projection variability was positive (mean difference \(=7.5\), \(95\%\) CI \([2.2,12.8]\), paired \(p=.008\); Figure~\ref{fig:optimization_validation}A). Thus, the model recovered a neural signal that remained behaviour-linked while changing more smoothly than RT itself.

We also tested whether the selected voxels had lower variability than a relevant motor-area comparison pool (Figure~\ref{fig:optimization_validation}B). Their mean consecutive-trial variability was \(2.50\), whereas size-matched samples from the non-selected motor pool had a mean variability of \(3.46\) and a \(95\%\) CI of \([2.76,4.38]\). The null sampling was matched by sample size only, not by hemisphere, ROI size, spatial location, or smoothness. 

Together, these analyses show that the model recovered a behaviour-linked neural projection with lower consecutive-trial variability than RT. This shows that a stable central vigour-related component can be detected in PD even when behaviour itself fluctuates.

\subsection{Spatial distribution of the optimized vigour-related network}
The optimized vigour network comprised \(8{,}182\) voxels and was distributed across both hemispheres (Figure~\ref{fig:main_vigour_network}). It was not limited to a single motor cluster. The largest parts of the map were in the temporal, parietal,
466 and the frontal cortex. A sensorimotor component was also present in precentral, postcentral, paracentral, and supplementary motor areas. Additional clusters extended into the orbitofrontal cortex, occipital cortex, fusiform and parahippocampal regions, hippocampus, amygdala, caudate, putamen, thalamus, and cerebellum (Figures ~\ref{fig:main_vigour_network} and  Supplementary Figure~S8).

Overall, the optimized map reflected a distributed network. It included motor-related regions, but the pattern was broader than primary movement output alone. The strongest spatial coverage involved temporal, parietal, and frontal regions, together with limbic, subcortical, and cerebellar areas.

\subsection{Limited overlap with the task-engagement reference map}
We compared the vigour network with the standard GLM task-activation map to test whether the optimized network was mainly a subset of general task activation (Figures~\ref{fig:main_network_task_overlap} and \ref{fig:roi_task_overlap_summary}). The two maps showed limited same-voxel overlap. The vigour network contained \(8{,}182\) voxels, whereas the task-activation map contained \(21{,}610\) voxels. Only \(835\) voxels were shared, giving a Dice coefficient of \(0.056\) and a Jaccard index of \(0.029\).

The task-activation map was broad and covered many regions involved in performing the task. In contrast, the vigour network was more selective. Some overlap appeared in motor-related regions, including pericentral cortex, supplementary/paracentral motor areas, putamen, cerebellum, and parietal cortex. However, several regions contained both maps with little or no same-voxel overlap, and temporal-limbic and orbitofrontal components were more prominent in the vigour network than in the task-activation reference (Figure~\ref{fig:roi_task_overlap_summary}; Supplementary Tables~S4 and S5).

\subsection{Medication-related effects on the vigour-network}
After defining the vigour network, we tested whether medication altered its functional connectivity. The topology-control analysis showed that connectivity patterns differed more between medication states than between repeated runs within the same state, for both Pearson-based and mutual-information-based edge patterns (Supplementary Figure~S9). This result indicates that the medication state changed interaction in the network beyond ordinary run-to-run variability. It should not be interpreted as evidence that ON medication simply stabilized every selected voxel.

We next asked whether medication-related reorganization occurred locally within ROIs or between distinct ROIs (Figure~\ref{fig:medication_local_distributed_fc}). Medication primarily increased local connectivity within ROIs of the vigour network. Intra-ROI FC increased significantly from OFF to ON medication (\(\Delta_{\mathrm{ON}-\mathrm{OFF}}=+0.0147\), \(p=0.006\)), whereas between-ROI FC showed no reliable change and remained close to zero (\(\Delta_{\mathrm{ON}-\mathrm{OFF}}=-0.0006\), \(p=0.723\)). This pattern suggests that dopaminergic medication strengthened local functional coupling within the vigour network rather than broadly increasing distributed connectivity between ROIs.

To assess whether this pattern was specific to the optimized network, we repeated the same analysis using the standard GLM task-activation map. The task-activation map also showed a significant difference between intra-ROI and between-ROI medication-related changes (\(p=0.012\); Figure~\ref{fig:medication_local_distributed_fc}). However, the underlying pattern was weaker and less specific than in the vigorous network. In the vigour network, the effect was driven by a robust increase in intra-ROI FC. In the task-activation map, intra-ROI FC showed only a non-significant increase (\(\Delta_{\mathrm{ON}-\mathrm{OFF}}=+0.0081\), \(p=0.108\)), together with a non-significant tendency toward reduced between-ROI FC (\(\Delta_{\mathrm{ON}-\mathrm{OFF}}=-0.0098\), \(p=0.142\)). Thus, task-active regions also captured a local-versus-distributed medication contrast, but the optimized vigour network more clearly identified medication-related strengthening of local coupling.

\subsection{GVS-related effects on the vigour-network}
We next asked whether GVS changed functional connectivity within the vigour network. For each active GVS condition, each edge was compared with the matched sham edge from the same subject, medication state, and run. Because each participant contributed multiple GVS blocks, observations were not fully independent. We therefore used this analysis to describe stimulation-sensitive edges rather than to make a strong therapeutic or group-level causal claim.

Using mutual information, active GVS produced \(156\) FDR-significant sham-referenced edge changes out of \(703\) tested edges. Of these, \(46\) increased and \(110\) decreased (Figure~\ref{fig:gvs_edge_changes}A). The strongest decreases involved left amygdala-left thalamus, left cerebellum-left putamen, and left cingulate-left thalamus. The strongest increases involved the left parietal cortex-left thalamus and the left putamen-left supplementary motor area. At the anatomical-sector level, decreases were most concentrated between limbic/olfactory and somatomotor/cerebellar regions, with additional decreases linking subcortical, cingulate-temporal, and somatomotor/cerebellar sectors.

Using Spearman's rank correlation, active GVS produced \(194\) FDR-significant edge changes (Supplementary Figure~S10). This pattern was more balanced, with \(103\) increases and \(91\) decreases. The strongest increases involved right cingulate-right temporal cortex, left fusiform cortex-left putamen, and left fusiform cortex-right precentral cortex. The strongest decreases involved right amygdala-left precentral cortex, right amygdala-left frontal cortex, and left hippocampus-left temporal cortex. The two metrics did not identify the same edge list, which is expected because they measure different forms of dependence. However, both analyses showed that GVS perturbed distributed interactions within the final network, repeatedly involving limbic/olfactory, medial-temporal, subcortical, visual, cingulate-temporal, and somatomotor/cerebellar components.

As a sanity check, we repeated the mutual-information analysis using the task-activation map instead of the optimized vigour network (Figure~\ref{fig:gvs_edge_changes}B). GVS also produced significant edge changes in this task-activation network. However, the detailed pattern was different: the task-activation map showed \(134\) significant mutual-information edges and \(164\) significant Spearman edges, with only \(14\) shared mutual-information edges and \(16\) shared Spearman edges overlapping the significant vigour-network edges. This supports a cautious interpretation: GVS affects task-active circuitry in general, but the optimized vigour-related network shows a partly distinct pattern of GVS-sensitive connectivity.

\section{Discussion}
This study asked whether trial-wise variability in motor vigour in PD reflects instability in a central vigour-related neural signal, or whether some behavioural variability arises downstream of a relatively stable central signal. The results support the second interpretation. Using fMRI during a ballistic hand-squeeze task, we identified a distributed neural projection that tracked RT while showing lower consecutive-trial variability than the behavioural signal itself. The selected voxels were also less variable than a broader motor-area comparison pool. The main finding is therefore not simply that RT was associated with brain activity. Rather, the study recovered a behaviour-linked neural expression of vigour in PD that was more stable than the behaviour it tracked.

This stability gap is important because behavioural variability alone cannot reveal where instability enters the motor system. In PD, response times fluctuate from moment to moment, and motor-unit firing can be more variable even when average performance is partly preserved \citep{macdonald2025capturing,wilson2020motor}. More generally, movement variability can arise from central preparatory states, adaptive exploration, and downstream motor noise \citep{churchland2006preparatory,faisal2008noise,harris1998signal,pekny2015reward,wu2014variability}. The present findings argue against a simple interpretation in which variable vigour directly reflects an unstable central vigour command. Instead, they suggest that a relatively stable central signal related to trial-wise vigour can still be recovered in PD, and that at least some behavioural fluctuation in movement initiation may arise downstream of this signal or through its interaction with later motor processes.

A potential concern is that this conclusion could appear circular because low consecutive-trial variability was one optimization criterion. However, the analysis did not merely impose stability and then report the same property as a result. The model was constrained not only by stability, but also by task engagement, behavioural relevance, and spatial coherence. We therefore tested the recovered solution against meaningful baselines. At the subject level, the optimized projection was less variable than RT. At the voxel level, the selected voxels were concentrated among low-variability voxels and showed lower mean variability than size-matched samples from non-selected motor-area voxels. These validation analyses show that the solution was not an arbitrary smooth sample of motor activity and was not simply a restatement of behavioural variability.

The spatial organization of the projection further supports this interpretation. The recovered vigour-related network included a motor-related core with contributions from pericentral cortex, superior parietal cortex, putamen, and cerebellar regions. This is consistent with prior work implicating motor cortical, basal-ganglia, thalamic, and cerebellar systems in movement speed, force, amplitude, and vigour \citep{anderson2012ecog,dudman2016basal,shirinbayan2019cortical,spraker2007basalganglia,turner2010basal,yttri2016opponent}. However, the projection was not limited to execution-related motor regions. It also included the amygdala, hippocampus, parahippocampal cortex, temporal cortex, frontal and orbitofrontal cortex, and striatum. A signal reflecting pure motor execution would be expected to localize more strongly to the primary motor and adjacent execution-related areas. In contrast, the present projection was enriched in regions positioned to integrate cue value, context, salience, motivational state, and action readiness.

This network organization fits the behavioural demands of the task. The putamen and broader basal ganglia are directly relevant to vigour because dopaminergic cortico-basal-ganglia circuits regulate movement velocity and energetic investment in action \citep{dudman2016basal,niv2007tonic,turner2010basal,yttri2016opponent,zenon2016dopamine}. Amygdala involvement is also plausible because primate amygdala neurons encode positive and negative visual-stimulus value during learning \citep{paton2006primate}, and anatomical evidence supports an amygdala--motor pathway through which affective or salient cues can shape action \citep{grezes2014direct}. Hippocampal and parahippocampal involvement may reflect contextual organization of action: reward behaviour depends on hippocampus--nucleus accumbens synaptic strength \citep{legates2018reward}, the hippocampus can organize upcoming skilled action sequences \citep{yewbrey2024hippocampus}, and hippocampal mechanisms support visually cued contextual response selection \citep{kim2012hippocampus}. Together, these findings suggest that the recovered network integrates motor preparation with the contextual and motivational signals that determine whether an action is initiated quickly on a given trial.

The limited overlap with the task-activation map strengthens this argument. A task-activation map asks where activity is reliably evoked by performing the task. The optimization asked a different question: which spatially coherent neural projection is linked to how quickly an action is initiated while maintaining a stable expression over consecutive trials? The low Dice and Jaccard values indicate that the network most consistently activated by the task is not the same as the network that best captures stable trial-wise vigour-related coding. This distinction matters because it separates mean task activation from trial-wise vigour coding. Vigour is therefore not simply a property of task-evoked activation amplitude; it is expressed through the organization of a distributed neural signal linking preparatory, motivational, contextual, and motor systems.

The medication analysis shows that the recovered vigour network was not fixed across dopaminergic state. Connectivity patterns within the network differed more between OFF and ON medication than between repeated runs within the same medication state, for both Pearson-based and mutual-information-based measures. More specifically, ON medication was associated with a selective increase in local within-ROI coupling, whereas between-ROI coupling remained close to zero and did not change reliably. This pattern was clearer in the optimized vigour network than in the standard task-activation reference map: the reference map showed a significant local-versus-distributed contrast, but its separate within-ROI and between-ROI changes were weaker and individually non-significant. Thus, the main medication-related finding is not a global increase in network connectivity, and not evidence that medication normalized the network. Rather, dopaminergic state was associated with stronger local coherence within the recovered vigour-related network. This interpretation is consistent with prior work linking dopamine to movement vigour and with evidence that levodopa can alter functional network organization in PD \citep{niv2007tonic,berman2016levodopa,zenon2016dopamine,mazzoni2007parkinson}.

This result also refines the central-versus-downstream interpretation. The main stability finding suggests that a relatively stable behaviour-linked neural projection can be recovered even when reaction time fluctuates from trial to trial. The medication finding adds that the network expressing this signal is state-sensitive: OFF and ON medication were associated with different patterns of functional coupling, especially within ROIs. Therefore, behavioural variability should not be assigned to a single source. Some variability may arise as a central vigour-related signal is transformed into motor output, but dopaminergic state may also affect the local network organization through which that signal is expressed. This conclusion should remain cautious, because the study did not directly test a causal pathway from medication to network coupling to behaviour, and the fixed OFF-then-ON session order leaves possible practice, fatigue, time-on-task, or scanner-drift confounds.

The GVS findings provide exploratory evidence that the recovered network is stimulation-sensitive. Active GVS produced sham-referenced connectivity changes within the vigour network, but the direction and anatomical distribution of these changes were heterogeneous across edges and depended on the connectivity metric. Mutual information identified more decreases than increases, whereas the correlation analysis showed a more balanced mixture of increases and decreases, and the two metrics did not identify the same edge set. The task-activation reference map also showed GVS-related edge changes, but with limited overlap with the vigour-network pattern. Thus, GVS appears to perturb task-related circuitry broadly while revealing a partly distinct set of stimulation-sensitive interactions within the optimized vigour network. The practical implication is that future stimulation studies should not only ask whether GVS changes mean activation in selected regions or restore a common network configuration. A more informative approach would test whether individualized stimulation parameters can reliably alter the stability, topology, or behavioural coupling of the vigour-related projection \citep{lee2021frequency,cai2018galvanic,menon2025individualising}.

Several limitations should guide interpretation. The study included only participants with PD, so the recovered network cannot yet be considered PD-specific; it may reflect a more general vigour-related signal that remains detectable in PD. Vigour was indexed mainly by reaction time, which captures response initiation but not other components such as movement speed, force amplitude, force rise time, or movement duration. Causal interpretation is also limited by the fixed OFF-then-ON medication order, which may confound medication effects with practice, fatigue, time-on-task, or scanner drift, and by the different fMRI acquisition protocol used for the first three participants. In addition, reward cues, GVS condition, block context, and adaptation across the run may have contributed to both RT and trial-wise neural activity, so the recovered projection should not be interpreted as a pure motor-vigour signal. Finally, the optimization was linear and was developed and characterized in the same dataset. Cross-validation and control analyses reduce this concern, but independent validation is needed in new PD cohorts, healthy adults, and different motor tasks.

Overall, the present study suggests that trial-wise vigour variability in PD should not be interpreted simply as instability of a central motor command. A relatively stable, behaviour-linked neural signal can be recovered, and this signal is organized across motor, striatal, limbic, contextual, and motivational systems. Behavioural variability may therefore partly arise downstream of central vigour-related coding, but dopaminergic state and vestibular stimulation also modulate the network through which this coding is expressed.

\section{Conclusions}
The present study identifies a distributed vigour-related neural projection in Parkinson's disease whose trial-wise expression is more stable than the behavioural reaction-time fluctuations it tracks. This projection is anatomically distributed, differs from standard task activation, and shows dopaminergic-state-related strengthening of local within-ROI coupling. Exploratory GVS analyses further show that the recovered network is perturbable by vestibular stimulation, although these effects are heterogeneous and should not be interpreted as evidence for a single therapeutic stimulation pattern. Together, the findings support a model in which PD-related variability in response initiation is not solely the product of an unstable central signal. Instead, a relatively stable central vigour-related signal can be recovered, while behavioural variability may arise partly as that signal is transformed into motor output under different dopaminergic and stimulation states.
