\section{Behavioural quality control and reaction-time summaries}
This section reports clinical summaries and behavioural quality-control information. Figure~\ref{fig:RT_information} summarises trial-level reaction-time quality control by participant, medication session, and run.

\begin{figure}[H]

    \caption{Reaction-time quality-control summary by participant, medication session, and run. Stacked bars show valid, missed, and invalid go trials; top and bottom panels show OFF- and ON-medication sessions, respectively. Within each participant, the left and right bars correspond to runs 1 and 2. The dashed horizontal line marks the expected number of go trials per run. Valid RT trials are go trials with finite positive RT values; missed trials are go trials flagged as no-squeeze events; invalid RT trials are go trials without finite RT values, indicating that the squeeze was not performed correctly. Grey bars mark run/session without corresponding fMRI data.}
    \label{fig:RT_information}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:RT_changes} summarises reaction-time patterns across medication conditions and task progression. Reaction times were modestly faster in the ON-medication condition than in the OFF-medication condition. This pattern was visible both in the trial-wise RT distributions, where the ON distribution was slightly shifted toward shorter latencies, and in the subject-paired means, where most participants showed lower mean RT in the ON-medication condition. The paired subject-level test showed a mean ON–OFF difference of $-13.6$ ms, $95\%$ CI $[-27.5, 0.3]$, $t(16) = -2.08$, p = $.054$, indicating a small medication-related speeding effect that approached but did not reach conventional significance. By comparison, temporal effects within the task were limited: RT was only slightly slower in run $2$ than run $1$, and trial position within blocks showed no clear monotonic practice or drift pattern. Overall, the behavioural results suggested a small medication-associated reduction in RT, with practice, fatigue, and within-task drift contributing only modestly to RT variation.

\section{Voxel-level BOLD variability across model conditions}
\label{app:voxel_bold_variability}
Because local stability was included as one component of the optimisation objective, we compared the voxel-level BOLD variability profile of the final map with two reference maps: the standard task-activation map and a control map obtained from the same optimisation framework after removing the low-variability penalty. For each voxel, variability was computed using the normalised consecutive-trial metric defined in Equation~4 of the main text, with \(x_i\) corresponding to that voxel's BOLD response on trial \(i\). The summation was restricted to adjacent valid trial pairs, excluding run and block boundaries. Voxel-level values were then grouped by map and summarised using a normalised histogram and empirical cumulative distribution function (Figure~\ref{fig:voxel_bold_variability}).

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{supplementary_figures/Sup_Figure17.pdf}
    \caption{Voxel-level consecutive-trial BOLD variability in the final motor-vigour network and task-activation maps. Variability was computed for each voxel using the normalised consecutive-trial metric defined in Equation~4 of the main text, after excluding adjacent trial pairs that crossed run or block boundaries. The left panel shows the normalised histogram of voxel variability values, and the right panel shows the empirical cumulative distribution function. Lower values indicate smaller local changes in voxel BOLD responses across adjacent valid trials.}
    \label{fig:voxel_bold_variability}
\end{figure}

The final motor-vigour network showed the strongest low-variability profile across the three maps. Compared with the task-activation map, its distribution was shifted toward lower consecutive-trial BOLD variability and contained a larger fraction of voxels in the very low-variability range. The no-variability-penalty control map showed an intermediate profile: its median variability was closer to the final motor-vigour network than to the task-activation map, but it contained fewer very low-variability voxels than the final network. Thus, the low-variability term mainly sharpened the lower tail of the selected-voxel distribution, increasing the concentration of locally stable BOLD voxels within a solution that was also constrained by task engagement, behavioural relevance, and spatial smoothness.

Summary statistics are reported in Table~\ref{tab:voxel_trial_variability}. The final motor-vigour network had the lowest median variability and the highest proportion of voxels with variability below \(0.01\). Directional voxel-level distributional comparisons showed lower variability in the final motor-vigour network than in the task-activation map, one-sided \(p=1.14\times10^{-5}\), and lower variability in the final motor-vigour network than in the no-variability-penalty control map, one-sided \(p=3.81\times10^{-6}\). By contrast, evidence for lower variability in the no-variability-penalty control map relative to the task-activation map was weak, one-sided \(p=0.135\). Because local stability contributed to the final optimisation objective and voxel-level measurements are spatially correlated, these comparisons were interpreted as supportive distributional summaries rather than as independent validation of the stability criterion.

\section{Anatomical characterisation of the vigour network}
\label{app:anatomy}
\subsection{Threshold Robustness Analysis}
\label{app:threshold_robustness}

We tested whether the definition of the vigour network depended strongly on the voxel-weight threshold. The threshold was varied across the voxel-weight distribution at p80, p85, p90, p95, and p97.5 (Figure~\ref{fig:threshold_robustness}). To summarise the anatomical composition of each thresholded map, suprathreshold voxels were assigned to Anatomical labels were assigned using the Automated Anatomical Labelling atlas 3, version 2 (AAL3v2; \citep{rolls2020automated}) labels and then grouped into broader bilateral anatomical regions. This grouping was applied only after thresholding and did not affect the voxel weights or the optimisation result.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{supplementary_figures/Sup_Figure6.pdf}
    \caption{Threshold robustness analysis for the vigour network. The voxel-weight threshold was varied across percentiles p80, p85, p90, p95, and p97.5. (A) Number of retained voxels at each threshold. (B) Retention and Jaccard overlap of anatomical regions relative to the p90 reference set. (C) Anatomical composition of each thresholded map, showing voxel counts by region and threshold.}
    \label{fig:threshold_robustness}
\end{figure}

The p90 threshold provided the best balance between reducing the voxel set and preserving the main anatomical pattern. At p90, the network contained \(8{,}182\) suprathreshold voxels. More liberal thresholds retained many more voxels (\(18{,}375\) at p80 and \(13{,}782\) at p85), but did not reveal a substantially different anatomical pattern. All p90 anatomical groups were also present at p80 and p85, and the Jaccard overlap with the p90 region set remained high at p85 (\(0.95\)). In contrast, stricter thresholds began to remove regions: p95 retained \(84.2\%\) of the p90 groups, whereas p97.5 retained only \(63.2\%\).

\subsection{ROI-wise composition of the vigour network}
The AAL3-derived atlas shown in Figure~\ref{fig:roi_atlas} was used to define ROIs in this study. After thresholding the final voxel-weight map at the 90th percentile, suprathreshold voxels were assigned to AAL3-derived bilateral anatomical groups. These ROI labels were used only for anatomical description and ROI-level signal extraction; they did not affect the optimisation weights or the definition of the voxel-weight map.

The olfactory cortex label should be interpreted cautiously. In this atlas-based summary, it refers to the AAL3 anatomical region around the ventral/anterior frontal base and should not be taken as evidence for odour-related processing.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{supplementary_figures/Sup_Figure7.pdf}
    \caption{AAL3-derived bilateral ROI atlas used for anatomical summaries and ROI-level signal extraction. Axial, coronal, and sagittal projections show the grouped anatomical labels used in the network, medication-state, and connectivity analyses. Colours mark the broad bilateral ROI groups used for visualisation and summary statistics.}
    \label{fig:roi_atlas}
\end{figure}

The vigour network is shown by the broad anatomical sector below. The ROI-wise profile showed that the vigour network was distributed across multiple anatomical systems rather than concentrated in a single motor region. In terms of total map composition, temporal cortex made the largest contribution (25.27\%), followed by parietal cortex (13.46\%), frontal cortex (11.38\%), cerebellum (7.90\%), and orbitofrontal cortex (7.22\%). These values describe how much each ROI contributed to the selected map, so larger anatomical regions can contribute a greater number of selected voxels.

To account for ROI size, we also examined the percentage of each ROI that was selected. This ROI-normalised view highlighted stronger enrichment in temporal-limbic and subcortical regions, including the amygdala (26.52\% of the ROI), parahippocampal gyrus (24.82\%), caudate (14.27\%), orbitofrontal cortex (12.60\%), hippocampus (10.66\%), paracentral lobule (10.46\%), and putamen (10.32\%). This pattern indicates that the vigour network was not simply driven by the largest cortical regions, but also included smaller regions with relatively high selected-voxel coverage.

Motor-related regions were also clearly represented, including the supplementary motor area, precentral gyrus, postcentral gyrus, paracentral lobule, putamen, parietal cortex, and cerebellum. However, these regions did not dominate the ROI-normalised profile. Overall, the ROI-wise composition supports the view that the vigour network contains a motor-related component, but is broader than primary movement execution alone, extending into temporal-limbic, orbitofrontal, associative cortical, cerebellar, and subcortical regions.

ROI summaries confirmed that the map included motor and non-motor components (Table~\ref{tab:roi_vigour_network} and Fig.~\ref{fig:supp_vigour_network_anatomy}). \subsection{Comparison with the task-activation map}
Tables~\ref{tab:two_networks} and \ref{tab:ablation_full_vs_task_only_roi} provide a compact summary of the limited overlap between the vigour network and the task-activation map, including both global overlap metrics and region-wise voxel counts. The task-activation map was used as a reference for conventional task-evoked activity. This comparison asked whether the vigour network was simply a smaller subset of voxels that were active during the hand-squeeze task. Because task engagement was included in the optimisation framework, some overlap with the task-activation map was expected. However, task engagement was only one part of the model; the final vigour network was also shaped by behavioural relevance, low consecutive-trial variability, and spatial coherence.

The voxelwise comparison showed that the two maps had limited same-voxel overlap. The task-activation map contained 21,610 voxels, whereas the vigour network contained 8,182 voxels. Only 835 voxels were shared, corresponding to 3.86\% of the task-activation map and 10.21\% of the vigour network. This produced a Dice coefficient of 0.056 and a Jaccard index of 0.029. The shared voxels were mainly located in regions expected to support task performance, including the putamen, cerebellar lobules, middle occipital cortex, paracentral lobule, superior parietal cortex, and postcentral gyrus. Thus, the vigour network retained a task-related motor and sensorimotor component, but most of its selected voxels did not coincide with the task-activation map.

The task-activation map showed broad coverage of task-responsive regions, including cerebellar, occipital, parietal, postcentral, insular, and putaminal areas. In contrast, the vigour network showed stronger relative representation in temporal-limbic and associative regions, including parahippocampal, amygdala, orbitofrontal, temporal, caudate, and hippocampal regions. Some ROIs contained voxels from both maps but showed little or no same-voxel overlap, indicating that the two maps could occupy the same broad anatomical region while selecting different local voxel patterns.

This distinction was also consistent with the activation-map comparison in Fig.~\ref{fig:model_spatial_similarity}. The standard GLM map and the two GLMsingle activation maps showed moderate overlap with one another, whereas each showed low Dice overlap with the vigour network. Together, these results indicate that the vigour network was not simply a selection of the task-activation map. Rather, it reflected a more selective voxel pattern related to the optimisation criteria used to identify the vigour projection.

\section{Detailed experimental and preprocessing methods}\label{app:methods_full}
This section contains procedural details.

\subsection{Full galvanic vestibular stimulation protocol}
Galvanic vestibular stimulation (GVS) was delivered continuously throughout each run using an STMISOLA constant-current stimulator (BIOPAC Systems Inc.) through $41$-mm Ag/AgCl electrodes placed bilaterally over the mastoid processes. Stimulation began before the first trial of the run. Electrode placement was verified using a $1$~Hz sinusoidal stimulus, and stimulation amplitude was individualised using a staircase procedure to determine the highest current below the threshold for superficial cutaneous sensation at the electrode site \citep{nguyen2022thresholds}. Participants were monitored throughout stimulation for discomfort or adverse effects.

Eight active amplitude-modulated waveforms were generated in MATLAB using suppressed-carrier modulation. The waveforms varied in carrier frequency ($75$ or $125$~Hz) and envelope frequency ($0$, $10$, $16$, or $25$~Hz). The resulting GVS stimuli were labelled as follows: GVS1, $75$~Hz carrier and $0$~Hz envelope; GVS2, $75$~Hz carrier and $10$~Hz envelope; GVS3, $75$~Hz carrier and $16$~Hz envelope; GVS4, $75$~Hz carrier and $25$~Hz envelope; GVS5, $125$~Hz carrier and $0$~Hz envelope; GVS6, $125$~Hz carrier and $10$~Hz envelope; GVS7, $125$~Hz carrier and $16$~Hz envelope; and GVS8, $125$~Hz carrier and $25$~Hz envelope. A sham condition, in which no current was delivered ($0$~mA), was also included. These frequencies were selected to approximate typical vestibular afferent firing rates and oscillatory bands implicated in motor planning and execution. All stimulus waveforms were generated with MATLAB’s `ammod` function using zero initial phase and zero carrier amplitude, producing suppressed-carrier amplitude modulation. Spectral analysis confirmed the expected frequency structure: for the modulated waveforms, the spectrum contained two symmetric sideband peaks around the carrier, located at \( f_c \pm f_e \), where \( f_c \) is the carrier frequency and \( f_e \) is the envelope frequency.


Each run was divided into nine blocks, with every block corresponding to a single stimulation condition: one of the eight active GVS waveforms or the sham condition. Each block contained $10$ trials, and the assigned stimulation condition was active throughout the full block, including the cue period, delay period, Go-signal period, and response period. All nine conditions were presented exactly once per run. At block transitions, the next stimulation condition began immediately, except after every third block, when a $20$-s rest period was provided before the next condition. The order of the nine blocks was randomised independently for each run, so that the sequence of conditions differed across runs, sessions, and subjects. Participants were not informed of the stimulation order. Because stimulation conditions were presented in a blocked sequence without a dedicated washout interval between all adjacent blocks, carryover effects between successive stimulation conditions cannot be fully excluded. Randomisation of block order was used to reduce systematic order effects, and GVS-related results were therefore interpreted cautiously.

\subsection{Full MRI acquisition and preprocessing protocol}
Structural and functional MRI data were acquired on a Philips $3$T Ingenia Elition X whole-body MRI scanner upgraded to an MR7700 system (Philips Healthcare, Best, The Netherlands) using a $32$-channel head coil. For each participant, a T1-weighted structural image was acquired, followed by two task-based fMRI scans, one for each run of the behavioural task.

T1-weighted images were acquired using a 3D MPRAGE sequence with the following parameters: flip angle = $8^\circ$, echo time (TE) = $3.6$~ms, repetition time/shot interval (TR) = $2400$~ms, inversion time = $950$~ms, $180$ slices, field of view (FOV) = $256 \times 256 \times 180$~mm$^3$, and spatial resolution = $1$~mm$^3$ isotropic. Images were acquired with a compressed SENSE factor of $3.6$, and the acquisition time was $3{:}42$~min.

Functional MRI data were acquired using echo-planar imaging (EPI). The functional acquisition protocol differed for the first three participants compared with the rest of the sample. The first three participants were scanned with a multi-echo EPI protocol, with flip angle = $77^\circ$, TE = $12$~ms, TR = $2000$~ms, $48$ slices, FOV = $240 \times 144 \times 240$~mm$^3$, spatial resolution = $3$~mm$^3$ isotropic, SENSE factor = $2$, multiband factor = $2$, and acquisition time = $14{:}22$~min. The remaining participants were scanned with a single-echo EPI protocol, with flip angle = $77^\circ$, TE = $30$~ms, TR = $1000$~ms, $51$ slices, FOV = $240 \times 153 \times 240$~mm$^3$, spatial resolution = $3$~mm$^3$ isotropic, SENSE factor = $2$, multiband factor = $3$, and acquisition time = $14{:}17$~min. To support distortion correction, two spin-echo EPI images were acquired with opposite fat-shift directions, anterior-posterior and posterior-anterior. These spin-echo images were acquired with flip angle = $77^\circ$, TE = $55$~ms, TR = $2650$~ms, $51$ slices, FOV = $240 \times 153 \times 240$~mm$^3$, spatial resolution = $3$~mm$^3$ isotropic, SENSE factor = $2$, multiband factor = $3$, and acquisition time = $5.3$~s.

Image preprocessing was performed in FSL \citep{smith2004advances}. Structural images underwent bias-field correction and brain extraction. Functional images were corrected for head motion and susceptibility-induced distortions, with distortion correction referring to the correction of susceptibility-related spatial distortions in the functional images using the spin-echo images acquired with opposite fat-shift directions. No initial functional volumes were discarded during preprocessing because the scanner automatically discarded four dummy volumes before the start of saved image acquisition. Functional images were then high-pass filtered using a $100$~s cutoff, brain extracted, denoised using ICA-AROMA \citep{pruim2015ica}, and spatially smoothed with a $5$~mm full-width at half-maximum Gaussian kernel. The preprocessed functional images were then registered to each participant's structural image and normalised to standard MNI152 space.

\subsection{Task-activation map}
To provide a brain map of task-evoked activation, we first ran a standard GLM using FSL FEAT version 6.00 \citep{jenkinson2012fsl}. This analysis was separate from the trial-wise GLM described below and was used only to generate the task-activation map shown in Supplementary Figure~S4.

For each run, all valid Go trials (Supplementary Figure~S1) were modelled with one event regressor. Event timing was defined relative to Go-signal onset, with a duration of $2$ s and an amplitude of $1$. This regressor was convolved with FSL’s double-gamma hemodynamic response function, and a temporal derivative was included.

The first-level contrast tested the positive effect of task activity. The two runs from each subject-session were then combined using a fixed-effects model. The resulting subject-session maps were entered into a higher-level mixed-effects model using FLAME stage 1 \citep{woolrich2004multilevel, beckmann2003general}. The group model included one mean regressor and tested whether the average task response was greater than zero across the $35$ available subject-sessions. Group Z-statistic maps were thresholded using clusters formed at $Z \geq 3.1$ with a corrected cluster significance threshold of $p \leq 0.05$ \citep{worsley200114}. The thresholded group map was used as the task-activation map (Supplementary Figure~S4).

\subsection{Trial-wise GLM estimation and beta preprocessing details}
Task-related BOLD responses for each trial were estimated using trial-wise general linear model (GLM) beta estimates with GLMsingle \citep{prince2022improving}, with HRF fitting, GLMdenoise \citep{kay2013glmdenoise}, and fractional ridge regression enabled. Analyses were performed separately for each subject-session. Within each subject-session, the two task runs were analysed jointly, while run identity was preserved in the design matrix and nuisance regressors.

The GLM design matrix included Go-signal onset regressors with a fixed trial duration of $2$ s, corresponding to the period during which participants performed the task. Catch trials were not modelled as separate regressors and were excluded from the analysis because participants did not squeeze the bulb on these trials ($630$ of $6{,}300$ trials across all datasets). Reward-condition labels were also not included in the GLM design. To restrict the analysis to relevant tissue, background, cerebrospinal fluid (CSF), and white-matter voxels were masked out, retaining only grey-matter voxels. The initial MNI grey-matter, non-CSF candidate mask contained $91{,}781$ voxels. The mean CSF time series for each run was included as a nuisance regressor.

% Before applying GLMsingle, high-DVARS trials were identified from the preprocessed BOLD time series. For each file, the DVARS metric was robustly standardised using a median absolute deviation (MAD)-based score ($z_i$). Trials with $z_i > 3$ were labelled as high-outlier trials. The number of trials removed by this criterion was capped at $15\%$ per file to avoid excessive loss of trials while removing the largest signal-change outliers.

HRF fitting used the default GLMsingle library of $20$ candidate HRFs, and the HRF yielding the highest variance explained was selected separately for each voxel. GLMdenoise derived additional nuisance regressors from low task-related-variance voxels using principal component analysis and selected the number of noise components by cross-validation. Fractional ridge regression was used to stabilise single-trial beta estimates. Shrinkage fractions from $0.05$ to $1.00$ in increments of $0.05$ were evaluated, and the optimal shrinkage level was selected separately for each voxel by cross-validation. Run-specific baseline terms were modelled using GLMsingle's run-wise polynomial regressors, including degree-0 intercepts and higher-order polynomial terms up to degree $\mathrm{round}(L/2) $, where $ L$ is the run length in minutes.

A second cleaning step was then applied to the GLMsingle beta estimates. To detect outlier beta values, beta estimates were robustly normalised within each subject-session using the session-level median and MAD:

\[
\beta_{\mathrm{norm}} = \frac{\beta - \mathrm{median}(\beta)}{\mathrm{MAD}(\beta)} .
\]

Beta values with $|\beta_{\mathrm{norm}}|$ above the 99.9th percentile were flagged as beta outliers. For each voxel, the beta-outlier fraction was computed as the proportion of trials flagged as outliers. Voxels were retained when this fraction was $\leq 0.5$ and excluded when more than $50\%$ of their trials were beta outliers. This majority-trial threshold was used to remove voxels with persistently unstable beta estimates, rather than voxels with occasional large responses.

This cleaning retained $91{,}701 \pm 54$ voxels on average. This corresponded to an average removal of $0.09\% \pm 0.06\%$. To assess whether this filtering disproportionately affected susceptibility-prone ventral regions, removal rates were summarised using atlas labels and ventral-position proxies. Average run-level removal was $0.20\%$ in frontal orbital cortex, $0.06\%$ in temporal pole, $0.76\%$ in inferior temporal gyrus, $0.25\%$ in the ventral bottom quartile, and $0.03\%$ in all other voxels. Thus, run-level cleaning removed very few voxels overall; removal was somewhat higher in inferior temporal and ventral-bottom voxels, but absolute removal rates remained below $1\%$ in all regions.

The cleaned outputs from all subjects, sessions, and runs were then used to define a shared voxel space for group-level analysis. The coverage threshold was applied across all concatenated trial observations from all subjects, sessions, and runs, rather than separately within each subject or session. This approach was used because the group-level analysis required a common MNI-space voxel feature set across trials. Applying separate subject-level masks would have produced different voxel sets across subjects, whereas the shared mask allowed all valid trial-wise beta estimates to be aligned into one group-level matrix. The threshold was also permissive: voxels were retained if they had finite beta estimates for at least $70\%$ of all trial observations, so a voxel was not excluded simply because it was missing or unstable in a minority of subjects, sessions, or runs.

The final group coverage mask contained $91{,}769$ voxels, yielding a final group beta matrix of $91{,}769$ voxels $\times$ $5{,}463$ retained trials (Supplementary Figure~S1). The filter excluded $12$ voxels from the $91{,}781$-voxel candidate mask, retaining $99.99\%$ of candidate voxels. Subsequent analyses used the concatenated cleaned beta data in this final group voxel space.

\section*{Appendix A: Optimisation derivation and model-selection details}\label{app:optimization_full}

A group-level optimisation procedure was used to identify a vigour network
(main Figure~\ref{fig:task_analysis_overview}B,C). The aim of the optimisation framework was to identify a voxel-weight vector that produced a projected neural signal related to trial-wise motor vigour while satisfying constraints on task engagement, spatial coherence, and low consecutive-trial variability. In voxel space, this projection is defined by a voxel-weight vector, \(\mathbf{w}^{\mathrm{org}}\), that combines voxel activity into one scalar signal at each sample. For a neural data matrix
\(\mathbf{X}^{\mathrm{org}} \in \mathbb{R}^{N_v \times N_s}\), where columns are samples, the projected signal is

\begin{equation*}
\begin{aligned}
\mathbf{y}^{\mathrm{org}}
&=
\left(\mathbf{w}^{\mathrm{org}}\right)^\top
\mathbf{X}^{\mathrm{org}},
\qquad
\mathbf{w}^{\mathrm{org}}
&\in \mathbb{R}^{N_v \times 1},
\mathbf{y}^{\mathrm{org}}
\in \mathbb{R}^{1 \times N_s}.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:generic_projection}
\end{equation*}

Here, \(N_v\) is the number of voxels and \(N_s\) denotes the number of time samples in the neural data matrix. In this study, the same projection can be applied to two forms of neural data: single-trial beta estimates and BOLD time courses:

\begin{equation*}
\begin{aligned}
\mathbf{y}_{\beta}^{\mathrm{org}}
&=
\left(\mathbf{w}^{\mathrm{org}}\right)^\top
\mathbf{X}_{\beta}^{\mathrm{org}},
\qquad
\mathbf{X}_{\beta}^{\mathrm{org}} \in \mathbb{R}^{N_v \times N_{\mathrm{tr}}},
\mathbf{y}_{\beta}^{\mathrm{org}}
&\in \mathbb{R}^{1 \times N_{\mathrm{tr}}}.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:y_beta_org}
\end{equation*}

\begin{equation*}
\begin{aligned}
\mathbf{y}_{\mathrm{BOLD}}^{\mathrm{org}}
&=
\left(\mathbf{w}^{\mathrm{org}}\right)^\top
\mathbf{X}_{\mathrm{BOLD}}^{\mathrm{org}},
\qquad
\mathbf{X}_{\mathrm{BOLD}}^{\mathrm{org}} \in
\mathbb{R}^{N_v \times N_{\mathrm{tp}}},
\mathbf{y}_{\mathrm{BOLD}}^{\mathrm{org}}
&\in \mathbb{R}^{1 \times N_{\mathrm{tp}}}.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:y_bold_org}
\end{equation*}

Here, $N_{\mathrm{tr}}$ is the number of trials, and \(N_{\mathrm{tp}}\) is the number of time
samples. \(\mathbf{X}_{\beta}^{\mathrm{org}}\) and
\(\mathbf{X}_{\mathrm{BOLD}}^{\mathrm{org}}\) were not separate models; they were two
representations of the neural data used by the same projection framework.

Although the framework is defined in voxel space, direct optimisation over all voxels was impractical because of the high dimensionality of the data and memory limitations. In addition, the task was not a simple motor task and included several task-related and non-motor processes. Therefore, reducing components that were unlikely to reflect the motor-vigour-related signal was important before optimisation. Because missing values remained after beta-estimate preprocessing, the neural data were reduced using expectation-maximisation principal component analysis (EMPCA) \citep{roweis1997algorithms}. An \(80\%\) cumulative explained-variance threshold initially retained $14$ components.

EMPCA provided voxel-to-PC weights,
\(\mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{pca}} \in \mathbb{R}^{N_v \times N_{\mathrm{pc}}}\). Both neural
data matrices were projected into this common PCA space:

\begin{equation*}
\begin{aligned}
\mathbf{X}_{\beta}
&=
\mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{pca}}^\top
\mathbf{X}_{\beta}^{\mathrm{org}},
&
\mathbf{X}_{\beta}
&\in \mathbb{R}^{N_{\mathrm{pc}} \times N_{\mathrm{tr}}},
\\[2pt]
\mathbf{X}_{\mathrm{BOLD}}
&=
\mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{pca}}^\top
\mathbf{X}_{\mathrm{BOLD}}^{\mathrm{org}},
&
\mathbf{X}_{\mathrm{BOLD}}
&\in \mathbb{R}^{N_{\mathrm{pc}} \times N_{\mathrm{tp}}}.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:pca_inputs}
\end{equation*}

The optimisation was then performed in PCA space using
\(\mathbf{w} \in \mathbb{R}^{N_{\mathrm{pc}} \times 1}\). In this space, the projected
beta and BOLD signals were

\begin{equation*}
\begin{aligned}
\mathbf{y}_{\beta}
&=
\mathbf{w}^{\top}
\mathbf{X}_{\beta},
&
\mathbf{y}_{\beta}
&\in \mathbb{R}^{1 \times N_{\mathrm{tr}}},
\\[2pt]
\mathbf{y}_{\mathrm{BOLD}}
&=
\mathbf{w}^{\top}
\mathbf{X}_{\mathrm{BOLD}},
&
\mathbf{y}_{\mathrm{BOLD}}
&\in \mathbb{R}^{1 \times N_{\mathrm{tp}}}.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:y_beta_bold}
\end{equation*}

The total loss was defined using these two projected signals: the beta projection was used
for the trial-wise beta-based terms, including behavioural relevance to motor vigour, and
the BOLD projection was used for the consecutive-trial variability term. After
optimisation, the PCA-space weights were projected back to voxel space as $\mathbf{w}^{\mathrm{org}} = \mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{pca}} \mathbf{w}$. The resulting voxel-space weight vector was used to define the vigour network.

The optimisation model was based on four assumptions about the neural representation of motor vigour. First, the vigour network should consist of voxels engaged during task performance. Second, the projected signal should encode behavioural information. Third, neighbouring voxels should show similar contributions, reflecting spatial coherence. Fourth, the selected signal should show low consecutive-trial variability. These four conditions were incorporated into the model one by one, as described below.

\textbf{Condition 1: The projected signal should come from task-engaged voxels.}

The first condition was that the projected signal should be generated mainly from voxels that were engaged during task performance. Each voxel contributes to $y_{\beta}$ through its beta activity and its corresponding weight in $w$. Therefore, if a voxel has weak task-related beta activation, assigning a small weight to that voxel would allow a highly task-engaged region to contribute strongly to the projected signal:

\begin{equation*}
\begin{aligned}
\min_{w} \sum_j \frac{w_j^2}{\left|X_{\beta,j}\right|}
&=
\min_{w} w^\top
\operatorname{diag}\!\left(\frac{1}{\left|X_{\beta}\right|}\right)w.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation*}
This gives the task-engagement loss term:
\begin{equation*}
\begin{aligned}
L_{\mathrm{task}}(w)
&= w^\top C_{\mathrm{task}}w,
&
C_{\mathrm{task}}
&=
\operatorname{diag}\!\left(\frac{1}{\left|X_{\beta}\right|}\right).
\end{aligned}
\end{equation*}

Thus, $C_{\mathrm{task}}$ favours voxels with stronger task-related beta responses and discourages the model from selecting voxels with weak task engagement.

\textbf{Condition 2: The projected signal should encode behavioural information.}

The second condition was that the projected neural signal should be behaviourally relevant. In this study, the target behavioural variable was reaction time. Therefore, the model should find $w$ such that $y$ is strongly associated with the behavioural variable $X_{\mathrm{behav}}$:

\begin{align*}
\max_w \operatorname{Corr}\!\left(X_{\mathrm{behav}}, y_{\beta}\right)^2
&=
\max_w \operatorname{Corr}\!\left(X_{\mathrm{behav}}, w^\top X_{\beta}\right)^2
\\[4pt]
&=
\max_w
\frac{
\left[(w^\top X_{\beta})X_{\mathrm{behav}}^\top\right]^2
}{
w^\top (X_{\beta}X_{\beta}^\top)w
}
\\[4pt]
&=
\max_w
\frac{
w^\top\left(X_{\beta}X_{\mathrm{behav}}^\top X_{\mathrm{behav}}X_{\beta}^\top\right)w
}{
w^\top\left(X_{\beta}X_{\beta}^\top\right)w
}
\\[4pt]
&=
\max_w
\frac{w^\top C_{RT} w}{w^\top C_{var} w},
\end{align*}
where
\begin{equation*}
C_{RT} = X_{\beta}X_{\mathrm{behav}}^\top X_{\mathrm{behav}}X_{\beta}^\top,
\qquad
C_{var} = X_{\beta}X_{\beta}^\top.
\end{equation*}

Here, $C_{RT}$ captures the component of the projected beta signal that is aligned with behaviour, while $C_{var}$ normalises the association by the variance of the projected beta signal. Since the final optimisation problem is written as a minimisation problem, this behavioural-relevance term is later included with a negative sign.

\textbf{Condition 3: The projected signal should come from a spatially coherent network.}

The third condition was that the selected pattern should form a spatially coherent network rather than a scattered set of isolated voxels. This meant that neighbouring voxels were encouraged to have similar weights. If $E$ denotes the set of neighbouring voxel pairs, this penalty can be written as

\begin{equation}
\min_w \sum_{(p,q)\in E} (w_p-w_q)^2.
\end{equation}
A spatial adjacency matrix was constructed from voxel anatomy, and its graph Laplacian was used to define the spatial smoothness penalty. This gives

\begin{equation}
L_{\mathrm{smooth}}(w) = w^\top C_{\mathrm{smooth}}w,
\qquad
C_{\mathrm{smooth}} = L_{\mathrm{graph}}.
\end{equation}

Thus, $C_{\mathrm{smooth}}$ enforces spatial smoothness across neighbouring voxels. When the optimisation is performed in PCA space, this spatial smoothness penalty is expressed in the same reduced space before optimisation.

\textbf{Condition 4: The projected signal should show low consecutive-trial variability.}

The fourth condition was that the selected signal should exhibit low consecutive-trial variability. Variability was therefore defined as the difference between consecutive trials, rather than as variance across the full time series. This distinction is important because variance captures the overall dispersion of a signal around its mean but does not account for trial order. As a result, two projected signals may have similar variance even if one evolves smoothly across trials while the other fluctuates sharply from one trial to the next. Consecutive-trial differences, in contrast, preserve the local temporal structure of the signal and quantify local changes between neighbouring trials.

This definition is motivated by the behavioural structure of motor vigour. Vigour on trial $i$ is unlikely to be statistically independent from vigour on trial $i+1$, because consecutive trials are shaped by similar physiological, attentional, and task-related states. Trials that occur close in time are therefore expected to be more similar than trials separated by longer intervals. Accordingly, a stable vigour-related neural signal would be expected to show fewer large unexplained jumps between consecutive trials. This reasoning is consistent with previous work on reaction-time variability showing that standard deviation alone does not capture temporal dependency, whereas measures such as lag-one autocorrelation and successive-trial differences reveal the local structure of performance fluctuations \citep{perquin2023temporal}.

A similar principle has been applied in fMRI research using mean squared successive difference (MSSD) and related measures, which quantify moment-to-moment changes in BOLD activity rather than dispersion around a global mean \citep{garrett2011importance, wehrheim2024reliability, nomi2018resting}. This approach is particularly appropriate for task fMRI, where the expected signal may change gradually over time, across task conditions, or between blocks. A global variance measure could incorrectly treat such slow task-related changes as variability, whereas a consecutive-difference penalty specifically targets local instability between consecutive trials. Thus, the low-variability term in the present model penalised large consecutive-trial changes in the projected signal while allowing slower task-related changes to remain. Boundaries between runs and blocks were excluded from this penalty because trials across these boundaries do not reflect continuous local temporal dynamics and may contain artificial discontinuities introduced by changes in run structure or task context.

The objective therefore minimised variability between consecutive trials:

\begin{align}
\min_w \sum_{i\in I_c}
\left\|y_{\mathrm{bold},i}-y_{\mathrm{bold},i+1}\right\|_2^2
&=
\min_w \sum_{i\in I_c}
\left(y_{\mathrm{bold},i}-y_{\mathrm{bold},i+1}\right)
\left(y_{\mathrm{bold},i}-y_{\mathrm{bold},i+1}\right)^\top
\nonumber\\[4pt]
&=
\min_w \sum_{i\in I_c}
\left(w^\top X_{\mathrm{bold},i}-w^\top X_{\mathrm{bold},i+1}\right)
\left(w^\top X_{\mathrm{bold},i}-w^\top X_{\mathrm{bold},i+1}\right)^\top
\nonumber\\[4pt]
&=
\min_w \sum_{i\in I_c}
w^\top
\left(X_{\mathrm{bold},i}-X_{\mathrm{bold},i+1}\right)
\left(X_{\mathrm{bold},i}-X_{\mathrm{bold},i+1}\right)^\top
w.
\end{align}

Here, $I_c$ denotes the set of valid consecutive-trial pairs after excluding run and block boundaries, and \(y_{\mathrm{bold}, i} \in R^{1 \times time\_points}\) is the window-BOLD signal in a trial. This gives the BOLD variability loss:

\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
L_{\mathrm{bold}}(w)
&= w^\top C_{\mathrm{bold}}w,
\\
C_{\mathrm{bold}}
&= \sum_{i\in I_c}
\left(X_{\mathrm{bold},i}-X_{\mathrm{bold},i+1}\right)
\left(X_{\mathrm{bold},i}-X_{\mathrm{bold},i+1}\right)^\top.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

Therefore, $C_{\mathrm{bold}}$ penalises large changes in BOLD activity between consecutive trials.

The same low-variability condition was also applied to the beta estimates:

\begin{align}
\min_w \sum_{i\in I_c}
\left\|y_{\beta,i}-y_{\beta,i+1}\right\|_2^2
&=
\min_w \sum_{i\in I_c}
\left(y_{\beta,i}-y_{\beta,i+1}\right)
\left(y_{\beta,i}-y_{\beta,i+1}\right)^\top
\nonumber\\[4pt]
&=
\min_w \sum_{i\in I_c}
\left(w^\top X_{\beta,i}-w^\top X_{\beta,i+1}\right)
\left(w^\top X_{\beta,i}-w^\top X_{\beta,i+1}\right)^\top
\nonumber\\[4pt]
&=
\min_w \sum_{i\in I_c}
w^\top
\left(X_{\beta,i}-X_{\beta,i+1}\right)
\left(X_{\beta,i}-X_{\beta,i+1}\right)^\top
w.
\end{align}
This gives the beta variability loss:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
L_{\beta}(w)
&= w^\top C_{\beta}w,
\\
C_{\beta}
&= \sum_{i\in I_c}
\left(X_{\beta,i}-X_{\beta,i+1}\right)
\left(X_{\beta,i}-X_{\beta,i+1}\right)^\top.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

Therefore, $C_{\beta}$ penalises large changes in beta values between consecutive trials.

Both BOLD and beta variability terms were included because they capture complementary sources of variability. The beta estimates reflect activation related to movement execution, so $L_{\beta}(w)$ constrains variability in the movement-related neural response. However, the task also includes other processes. These sources of variability can be encoded in the BOLD signal even when they are not fully captured by movement-related beta estimates. Therefore, $L_{\mathrm{bold}}(w)$ was also included to account for broader task-related variability encoded in the brain.

\textbf{General loss function:} After defining the four conditions, the penalty terms were combined into a single total loss. The total penalty was defined as

\begin{align}
L_{\mathrm{total}}(w)
&= \alpha_{\mathrm{task}} L_{\mathrm{task}}(w) + \alpha_{\mathrm{BOLD}} L_{\mathrm{bold}}(w) + \alpha_{\beta} L_{\beta}(w) + \alpha_{\mathrm{smooth}} L_{\mathrm{smooth}}(w)
\nonumber = w^\top C_{\mathrm{total}}w,
\end{align}
where
\begin{equation}
C_{\mathrm{total}}
= \alpha_{\mathrm{task}} C_{\mathrm{task}} + \alpha_{\mathrm{BOLD}} C_{\mathrm{bold}} + \alpha_{\beta} C_{\beta} + \alpha_{\mathrm{smooth}} C_{\mathrm{smooth}}.
\end{equation}

The parameters $\alpha_{task}$, $\alpha_{BOLD}$, $\alpha_{\beta}$, and $\alpha_{smooth}$ control the relative contributions of task engagement, BOLD variability, beta variability, and spatial smoothness, respectively.

The final objective combined this total penalty with behavioural relevance. The model minimised the regularisation penalties while maximising the association between the projected beta signal and reaction time:

\begin{equation}
\min_w
\frac{\gamma L_{total}(w) - L_{RT}(w)}{L_{var}(w)} = \frac{\gamma w^\top C_{\mathrm{total}}w - w^\top C_b w}{w^\top C_d w}
\quad
\mathrm{s.t.}\ \|w\|_2=1.
\end{equation}

Here, $\gamma$ controls the balance between satisfying the regularisation criteria and maximising behavioural relevance. The denominator $w^\top C_d w$ normalises the objective by the variance of the projected beta signal.

The resulting projection was defined as
\begin{equation}
y = w^\top X_{\beta}.
\end{equation}

The optimisation problem was solved under the unit-norm constraint on $w$ using the Sequential Least Squares Programming (SLSQP) algorithm \citep{nocedal2006numerical, kraft1988software}. The objective combined a behavioural relevance term with four regularisation terms. The coefficients $\alpha_{\mathrm{task}}$, $\alpha_{\mathrm{BOLD}}$, $\alpha_{\beta}$, and $\alpha_{\mathrm{smooth}}$ controlled the relative contribution of the task-engagement, BOLD consecutive-trial variability, beta consecutive-trial variability, and spatial smoothness penalties, respectively. The scalar $\gamma$ controlled the overall strength of the combined penalty terms relative to the behavioural relevance term. Hyperparameters were selected using a coarse-to-fine cross-validation procedure. In the coarse stage, the model was evaluated over a predefined set of candidate penalty-weight combinations chosen to span weaker, balanced, and stronger weighting of the different penalties. Trials were divided into contiguous folds, and for each candidate setting the model was fit on the training folds and evaluated on the held-out fold using the absolute correlation between the optimised neural projection and behaviour. After the best-performing coarse setting was identified, a fine search was performed by testing small multiplicative perturbations around that setting, both for individual penalty weights and for the overall balance of all penalty weights. The final hyperparameter setting was chosen based on held-out correlation, with preference given to settings that showed stable performance across folds and small train-test differences. For each fold, the optimisation was then solved independently using the training data, and the resulting weights were projected back from PCA space to voxel space. Final voxel maps and projected signals were obtained by averaging across folds. The resulting weight assigned to each voxel reflected its contribution to satisfying the combined criteria of task engagement, behavioural relevance, spatial smoothness, and low variability. Voxels with the largest weights were then defined as the vigour network.

Finally, we defined the vigour network as voxels whose weights exceeded the 90th percentile. We chose this threshold because the 90th percentile provided a conservative but stable compromise between spatial specificity and anatomical coverage (Supplementary Figure~S6). Lower thresholds, such as the 80th or 85th percentiles, retained substantially more voxels and therefore produced a broader, less selective network. In contrast, more stringent thresholds, particularly the 95th and 97.5th percentiles, sharply reduced the number of retained voxels and began to exclude several anatomical regions. The p90 threshold preserved the main anatomical groups while avoiding the broader p80/p85 maps and the regional loss seen at p95/p97.5. It is an appropriate cutoff for defining the vigour network.

\section{Medication-state control analyses}
\subsection{Control analysis: medication-related connectivity-pattern changes exceeded run-to-run variation}

To test whether medication-related changes in the vigour-network connectivity pattern were larger than run-to-run variation, we compared within-state run~1-run~2 distances with OFF-ON medication distances for each participant. OFF-ON distances were larger than within-state run-to-run distances for both Pearson-correlation and mutual-information patterns (Pearson: \(d = 1.15\), \(p = 0.0006\); mutual information: \(d = 0.69\), \(p = 0.0015\); Figure~S9). This suggests that the network connectivity pattern changed with dopaminergic medication more than would be expected from short-term measurement fluctuation alone.

\subsection{Control analysis: medication-related FC changes relative to run-to-run baseline variability}
To test whether the medication-related within-versus-between-ROI FC pattern reported in main Figure~6 exceeded ordinary short-term variability, we repeated the same subject-level FC-change analysis using within-session run-to-run differences as a baseline and compared these values with the between-session medication-effect estimate (Figure~\ref{fig:medication_fc_run_to_run_baseline}). In the vigour network, medication-related between-session change was larger than run-to-run variability for intra-ROI FC (\(p=0.005\)) and for the intra-minus-between contrast (\(p=0.022\)), but not for between-ROI FC (\(p=0.961\)). In the task-activation map, between-session change was larger than run-to-run variability for intra-ROI FC (\(p=0.011\)) and between-ROI FC (\(p=0.015\)), with weaker evidence for the intra-minus-between contrast (\(p=0.059\)). Thus, the baseline analysis supported medication-related FC changes beyond short-term measurement fluctuation, with the local-versus-distributed specificity clearest in the vigour network.
