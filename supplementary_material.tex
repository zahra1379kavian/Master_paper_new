\section{Behaviour Information}
Behaviour trial flow and quality-control summary. Each subject/session/run has a total.

\begin{figure}[!htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{supplementary_figures/Sup_Figure25.pdf}
    \caption{Reaction-time quality-control summary by participant, medication session, and run. Stacked bars show valid, missed, and invalid go trials; top and bottom panels show OFF- and ON-medication sessions, respectively. Within each participant, the left and right bars correspond to runs 1 and 2. Each run contained 90 trials, including 9 catch trials not shown here and 81 go trials, marked by the dashed horizontal line. Valid RT trials were go trials with finite positive \(1/\mathrm{RT}\) values; missed trials were go trials flagged as no-squeeze events. Gray bars indicate data without corresponding fMRI data.}
    \label{fig:RT_information}
\end{figure}


Reaction times were modestly faster in the ON-medication condition than in the OFF-medication condition. This pattern was visible both in the trial-wise RT distributions, where the ON distribution was slightly shifted toward shorter latencies, and in the subject-paired means, where most participants showed lower mean RT ON medication. The paired subject-level test showed a mean ON-OFF difference of $-13.6$ ms, $95\%$ CI $[-27.5, 0.3]$, $t(16) = -2.08$, p = $.054$, indicating a small medication-related speeding effect that approached but did not reach conventional significance. By comparison, temporal effects within the task were limited: RT was only slightly slower in run $2$ than run $1$, block position showed a mild increase in RT consistent with weak fatigue or time-on-task effects, and trial position within blocks showed no clear monotonic practice or drift pattern. Overall, the behavioural results suggest a small medication-associated reduction in RT, with practice, fatigue, and within-task drift contributing only modestly to RT variation.

\section{Anatomical characterization of the vigour network}
\label{app:anatomy}
\subsection{Threshold Robustness Analysis}
\label{app:threshold_robustness}

We tested whether the final network definition depended strongly on the voxel-weight threshold. The threshold was varied across the voxel-weight distribution at p80, p85, p90, p95, and p97.5 (Figure~\ref{fig:threshold_robustness}). To summarize the anatomical composition of each thresholded map, suprathreshold voxels were assigned to AAL3v2 labels and then grouped into broader bilateral anatomical regions. This grouping was applied only after thresholding and did not affect the voxel weights or the optimization result.

\begin{figure}[!htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{supplementary_figures/OptimizationValidation_thresholds.pdf}
    \caption{Threshold robustness analysis for the optimized vigour-related network. The voxel-weight threshold was varied across percentiles p80, p85, p90, p95, and p97.5. The panels show the number of retained voxels, overlap with the p90 region set, and anatomical composition of each thresholded map.}
    \label{fig:threshold_robustness}
\end{figure}

The p90 threshold provided the best balance between reducing the voxel set and preserving the main anatomical pattern. At p90, the network contained \(8{,}182\) suprathreshold voxels. More liberal thresholds retained many more voxels (\(18{,}375\) at p80 and \(13{,}782\) at p85), but did not reveal a substantially different anatomical pattern. All p90 anatomical groups were also present at p80 and p85, and the Jaccard overlap with the p90 region set remained high at p85 (\(0.95\)). In contrast, stricter thresholds began to remove regions: p95 retained \(84.2\%\) of the p90 groups, whereas p97.5 retained only \(63.2\%\).
